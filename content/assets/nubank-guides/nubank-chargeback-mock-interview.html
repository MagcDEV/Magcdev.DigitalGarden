Mock interview: the chargeback platform, minute by minute

A full simulated system design round for the Nubank Lead role, with every interviewer question, every candidate answer, and what goes on the whiteboard at each step. Interruptions arrive roughly every ten minutes, the way candidates report them.

Companion to The chargeback platform, end to end. Read the answers once, then cover them and run it with a timer. 6 September 2026; the numbers, a pushback exchange and the bad-day questions were added 29 September 2026 from Nubank's own candidate PDF. Re-run 1 October 2026 in the four steps of the video Nubank's interviewers recommend (see The Hello Interview method, applied to Nubank's hour): the road map is said at minute two, the features and the qualities are written in its form, the one-table version is named and not drawn, the first version is on the board by minute 16, and the candidate chooses the deep dives.

Before you start

Two speakers: I is the interviewer, an engineer from a core-banking team; C is you. The boxes marked Whiteboard show what should be on the shared board at that moment, drawn as you talk. They are drawn here in the same style as the guides' diagrams, with colour for the consistency line; on the real board you draw the same boxes and arrows in one pen and say the colours out loud. Timestamps are where you should be, not where you must be; if you're five minutes behind at the drawing, cut a dive to a sentence, never the failures.

The round is run in the four steps of the video Nubank's interviewers recommend, and the candidate names each step as they enter it: requirements (features, then the scale question, then the qualities), entities and API, a high-level design that serves only the features, and deep dives one quality at a time. Two rules from that video shape the script. The high-level design starts at the rung a senior candidate starts at: the one-table version is named in a sentence and never drawn, because a design that cannot keep audit, retries or deadlines "would not pass at any level". And the drawing is on the board by about minute 16, so that more than half the hour is deep dives, which is where a Lead is graded.

The candidate's answers below are deliberately spoken, not written: short sentences, one idea each, said while drawing. That is the register to practise.

0:00

Interviewer

Thanks for joining. We've got about 55 minutes. I'd like you to design a card dispute platform for us. Customers dispute card transactions, we handle evidence, deadlines, submission to the card network, provisional credits, and the outcome has to be audited. Multi-country. Take it wherever you think it should go; I'll interrupt when I have questions.

Candidate

Great. Here's how I'll use the time, so you can interrupt wherever you like: requirements first, the features, then rough numbers, then the qualities with a number each; then the nouns and the operations on them; then a simple design that serves the features, drawn by about minute fifteen; then I'll go back to the qualities one by one and harden it.

So, requirements. Before I draw anything I want to pin the boundary, because this prompt can mean three different systems. Let me ask five things, quickly.

First: are we the issuer, the cardholder's bank, or the acquirer, the merchant's bank? I'll assume issuer, since the customer opens the dispute with us, unless you tell me otherwise.

Interviewer

Issuer. We're the customer's bank.

Candidate

Second: which countries and networks are in scope for the first version? Each combination changes reason codes, deadlines and evidence rules. I'd like to design so the variation lives in versioned policy and adapters, but I want to know whether you expect one or many on day one.

Interviewer

Brazil and Mexico, Visa and Mastercard. But design so Colombia is easy.

Candidate

Understood. Third: who owns the rules? I'll assume Legal and compliance own the meaning of eligibility and deadlines, and engineering implements them as versioned, testable policy where every decision records which version it used. Fourth: does opening a case ever move money by itself, or is provisional credit a separate policy decision? I'll treat it as separate: opening creates a case; credit is a policy outcome that may or may not follow.

Interviewer

Both of those match how we think about it. What's the fifth?

Candidate

What already exists. I'm going to assume there's a ledger that owns money, a card transaction store, identity, object storage and a notification service. This platform integrates with them; it doesn't rebuild them. In particular, this system never moves money itself. It asks the ledger to.

And one shape question: is the exchange with the network a real-time API, or scheduled files? Both exist in practice. I'll design for the real-time shape and show how a file changes it if you'd like.

Interviewer

Start real-time. We may come back to files.

Candidate

Then my boundary sentence is: this is a case-management system for the issuer side. It coordinates four things, customer intake, evidence, deadlines, and messages to the card network, plus money actions it asks the ledger to perform. Case acceptance is synchronous; everything external, network, ledger, documents, is asynchronous with an explicit Processing state. It is not the ledger and it is not the network.

Whiteboard, minute 4

3:30

Features as expectations, then the "below the line" list, said to get a nod. The video Nubank recommends writes requirements this way; its point is agreement on a boundary, not completeness.

Candidate

Then the features, as what each user expects. One: a customer disputes a card transaction and can see where it stands. Two: an operator reviews evidence and decides, under the policy for that country and network. Three: the platform submits the dispute to the network and records the outcome. Four: the platform asks the ledger for provisional credit, and for its reversal or adjustment once the outcome is known.

Below the line for today: fraud scoring, the acquirer and merchant side, the network's internals, and reporting dashboards, which I'd come back to if we have time. Does that boundary work for you?

Interviewer

It does. Reporting we can skip.

4:30

Numbers before the qualities, and before boxes. The video asks the interviewer for scale before writing a single non-functional requirement, because the qualities are written in terms of the numbers; Nubank's own prep document grades whether the arithmetic leads to a decision.

Candidate

Before I write the qualities I want the scale, because they'll be written in terms of it. What volume should I design for? If you don't have a number, may I assume something like a hundred million customers and around two billion card transactions a month?

Interviewer

Close enough. Go on.

Candidate

A dispute rate around a tenth of a percent gives two million disputes a month, roughly seventy thousand a day. A day is about a hundred thousand seconds, so that's under one case per second on average, call it eight at peak. Twenty events per case at a kilobyte each is forty gigabytes of events a month, half a terabyte a year. Evidence is the big one: two files at two megabytes per case is eight terabytes a month, which goes to object storage, never the database. And if thirty percent of cases need a human who does forty a day, that's about five hundred reviewers.

So the decision the numbers force: this is not a throughput problem. One well-indexed relational database handles it and would still handle ten times this. It is a correctness, deadline and people problem. That's where I'll spend the design: audit, durable deadlines, safe money intent, reconciliation, and measuring what to automate.

And my scope: the issuer-side dispute workflow with provisional credit, because that's where correctness is hardest. The network is an adapter, the ledger is an existing system, and I'll come back to reporting and analytics only if we have time.

Whiteboard, minute 5 (side notes)

5:30

The qualities, in context and quantified, then the same qualities as rules. The video calls a bare list of "scalable, low latency, fault tolerant" wholly uninteresting; each one here says what it means for this system and carries a number from the board.

Candidate

Now the qualities, each for this system and with a number. Auditable: any case reproducible as it stood on any date, from about twenty events per case. Deadlines kept: a legal window never missed silently, with alerts before and after breach, over about six million deadline rows a month. Money idempotent: a retry never credits twice. Available for intake: opening and reading a case keep working when the network or the ledger is down, with everything external shown as Processing. Latency: acceptance under a second, the operator's view under three hundred milliseconds, nothing external on that path. Scale: under one write a second, so one store is fine at ten times; Operations, at five hundred reviewers, is the real ceiling. Evidence trustworthy: a document is evidence only after its checks, eight terabytes a month in object storage.

And the same qualities as the rules I'll judge the design against, because that's the form a failure will test them in.

One: a case only moves through valid transitions under a versioned policy. No jumping from opened to closed unless a rule permits it, and an override needs a role, a reason and an approval, recorded.

Two: every action records who, why and when. There is no anonymous status change. The audit trail is the event history, not a log we bolt on.

Three: deadlines are durable. A due date is a saved row that cannot disappear if every worker dies. Not an in-memory timer.

Four: every money action is separately idempotent. A credit, a reversal, an adjustment: each has its own stable key, so a retry never credits twice, and one case can legitimately have several.

Five: network submissions reuse a stable external reference on every retry. A fresh reference could open a second dispute at the network.

Six: evidence is versioned, access-controlled and retained by policy. A replaced document is a new version linked to the old; the old bytes are never overwritten.

Seven: every decision records the policy version that evaluated it, so deploying a new rule can't silently change the meaning of an old decision.

The trade I'm making up front: this workflow is slow compared to authorisation, weeks not milliseconds, so I'll spend nothing on raw speed and everything on deadline, audit and money correctness.

Whiteboard, minute 7

8:00

Candidate

Six nouns, and I'll resist adding more.

DisputeCase is the current summary: state, amount, country, network, reason code, owner, policy version. CaseEvent is one immutable fact in its history: opened, evidence added, submitted, acknowledged, won. Deadline is a row with a type and a status. EvidenceItem is metadata pointing at a document in object storage: object version, checksum, scan status, uploader. NetworkSubmission is the stable reference we sent and whether it was acknowledged. FinancialAction is one money step with its own ledger key.

The case is the aggregate root. Everything changes through commands. Nobody edits a status column.

And the operations on them, three, because every change is a command on the case rather than an endpoint per field. Open a case: transaction, reason, amount, and an idempotency key the app mints at the tap; it returns the existing case on a replay. Run a command on a case: the command type, the expected case version, a reason, a key; it returns the accepted state by name, or a version conflict, or a policy rejection with the version that rejected it. Read a case: the snapshot, and the history as of a date on request. Evidence bytes go through a staged upload; a later command links the exact object version. Who is acting comes from the token, never the body. Ops search is a projection read, not part of this API.

Interviewer

Why both events and a snapshot? Why not just the case row with a status?

Candidate

Because a status row can only tell you where the case is, never how it got there or who did it, and this system exists to answer "how did it get there" to an auditor or a regulator. The events are the truth; the snapshot is a materialised current view so reads are one row instead of a replay. They're written in the same commit, and I can verify or rebuild the snapshot from the events at any time. It's event history plus a current aggregate, not full event sourcing where every read replays.

Whiteboard, minute 10

11:00

First interruption, arriving before the architecture is drawn. Take it, then return.

Interviewer

Quick one. A customer taps "dispute" twice because the app was slow. Two cases?

Candidate

No, and I need two mechanisms, not one. Request idempotency handles the literal retry: the app generates a key once per tap-intent and sends the same key on the retry; the platform returns the existing case. But two separate taps are two different keys, so I also need a business uniqueness rule: for this transaction, this claimant, this reason family, while a case is live, return the existing case or a deterministic conflict. I don't use transaction id alone, because partial disputes on one transaction are legitimate.

Let me get back to the step I was on: the entities and the API are done, so the drawing is next.

13:00

Begin simple, then iterate, from the rung a Lead starts at. The prep document wants a first idea and its flaws found by you; the video sets the floor: a design that cannot serve the features "would not pass at any level", and a senior candidate "starts a little further on". So the one-table version is named in one sentence, not drawn, and the first version on the board is the one that works.

Candidate

Step three, the design. I'll say the simplest version and not draw it: one dispute service, one Postgres with a cases table and a status column, synchronous calls to the network and the ledger, a cron that checks deadlines hourly. At under one case a second it would run for a week, and then it fails the qualities I just wrote: a status column can't tell an auditor who moved the case or why; a synchronous network or ledger call inside the transaction means a timeout leaves the case not matching reality and a retry can credit twice; the cron is the only guardian of a legal deadline; and two operators on one row is last-write-wins. So the first version I draw is the one that fixes those four, and I'll say what it costs as I go.

Candidate

Here's the first version, the write path. Every change is a command. A command is authenticated and authorised. I check its idempotency key and the case version it expects. I validate the transition against the current policy version for that country and network. Then, in one commit, I write the new event, the updated snapshot and version, any deadlines created or completed, the idempotency record, and outbox rows for every side effect this transition implies. I return the accepted state by name, "submitted", "provisional credit pending", not an HTTP 200.

Side effects happen after commit. Workers read the outbox and call the card network, the ledger, object storage, notifications. Whatever they observe comes back into the case as a new event: network acked, ledger posted transaction 9182, scan passed. So the case's own history says what actually happened outside it.

Two more pieces. Deadlines: a timer wakes workers near the due time, and an independent sweeper periodically queries the deadline rows for anything due or near due and catches whatever the timer missed. Search: Operations finds cases by country, age, reason, deadline through an index built from events; it may lag; Ops opens the authoritative case by id before acting.

What it costs, so it's said: two writes per command instead of one, a worker fleet, and explicit in-flight states the product has to show. I pay that for a rebuildable audit trail and money that can't be credited twice.

And what it doesn't handle yet, which is my menu for the rest of the hour. The timer alone finds deadlines, so I owe you the sweeper and the claim. One money step per case, so I owe you the keys for reversals and partial wins. Bytes in storage aren't evidence yet. The network is an HTTP call, and in practice it may be a file. No bad-day story yet, and no rollout. Of those I'd go deepest on provisional credit, because money meets long-running state there, and on failures, unless you'd rather steer me somewhere else.

Whiteboard, minute 16 (the first version drawn)

Interviewer

Why not use a workflow engine for all of this? We have one.

Candidate

I'd use it for what it's good at: timers, retries, orchestration of the steps. But the domain service still owns what a valid transition is, which policy version applies, the audit trail and the idempotency. The engine is execution infrastructure. The moment critical policy exists only as opaque workflow configuration, I can't test it, version it, or explain a decision from six months ago. So: engine underneath, domain rules on top.

Interviewer

And what exactly have you committed before you tell the customer "submitted"?

Candidate

The event, the snapshot and version, the relevant deadlines, the idempotency record, and the outbox row that says "submit this". So "submitted" means "durably intended", and the case can't say submitted while forgetting that the submission still has to happen: the promise to do it is in the same commit as the state that claims it. What I have not done is wait for the network to answer. That comes back as an event.

21:00

Second interruption.

Interviewer

Let's say a regulator asks: show me this case exactly as it stood on the third of June, including which rule you applied that day.

Candidate

Replay the case's events up to that timestamp and show the snapshot they produce. Every event carries actor, reason, time, and the policy version in force for that decision, so "which rule applied" is in the events, not reconstructed from a deployment history. This is a query, not a project, and it's the reason I refused a mutable status row ten minutes ago.

Interviewer

Good. You said provisional credit. Go.

24:00

Candidate

Deep dive one, provisional credit. The quality it serves is money idempotent under retry, with partial wins. It's where long-running case state meets customer money, and where the case model and the ledger have to agree without a distributed transaction.

Walkthrough. Ana disputes R$300. Policy for her product and country says she's owed provisional credit. The case commit writes a FinancialAction with key CB-812, provisional credit, v1, and an outbox row. Case state is under review; financial state is provisional credit pending. After commit, a worker sends that command to the ledger with that key. The ledger posts a balanced pair, Ana plus 300, a disputes clearing account minus 300, and returns its transaction id. The worker records provisional-credit-posted as a case event carrying that id. Ana sees the money.

Now the outcome. If she wins in full, finalise: the credit stands. If she loses, a new action, reverse provisional v1, asks the ledger for a linked reversal; the original posting is untouched. If she wins R$200 of 300, one adjustment for the difference, final adjustment v2, minus 100. Never edit the credit amount.

The case closes only when both are true: the workflow decision is recorded, and every required ledger action is confirmed. And at the end I reconcile three truths: the network's award, our recorded decision, and the ledger's postings. They must agree on outcome and amount.

Whiteboard, minute 28

Interviewer

Why is the key that specific? Why not just the case id?

Candidate

Because one case legitimately needs several different money actions. If the key were the case id, the reversal would look like a retry of the credit and the ledger would correctly refuse it. The key names the business action being retried: this case, this action, this version. The ledger enforces uniqueness on it, so a retried credit returns the prior posting and a reversal is recognisably a different thing.

Interviewer

The ledger call times out. Did the credit post?

Candidate

I don't know, and I don't guess. A timeout means unknown. I retry the exact same key. The ledger either returns the transaction it already posted, or posts it once now. The case stays in settlement-pending until I've observed a durable result. What I never do is treat the timeout as failure and issue a new key; that's how a customer gets credited twice.

Interviewer

She spends the R$300, then loses.

Candidate

The reversal may leave her negative. Whether that's a debt, a negative available balance, or a hardship arrangement is product and regulatory policy per country, and I need that decided before launch, not at 3 a.m. Technically: record the loss, ask the ledger for the linked reversal, present a clear repayment experience. Risk can cap provisional credits or delay availability, but that's a business lever. The system can't take money that isn't there and I shouldn't design as if it could.

33:00

Third interruption: the scalability push. This is where a Lead candidate reportedly failed.

Interviewer

Volume goes up tenfold. What breaks first, and how do you improve performance?

Candidate

Tenfold what, is my first question: cases per day, documents per case, or operators? I'll take cases per day, and I'll answer in the order I'd actually look.

First the arithmetic, because it decides the answer. Tenfold cases is seven hundred thousand a day: about eight writes a second average, eighty at peak, four hundred gigabytes of events a month, eighty terabytes of evidence a month, and five thousand reviewers if we automate nothing. So the database is still not the problem. Evidence retention becomes a real cost line, and Operations is the ceiling by an order of magnitude.

What I'd measure: p99 of the command path, queue age per dependency, oldest unprocessed deadline, outbox lag, index lag.

What scales by adding machines: every worker and adapter, because every side effect is idempotent and every claim is conditional. The search index, because it's a rebuildable projection. Evidence, because object storage is effectively unbounded and scanning has its own queue.

What the case store does: it partitions cleanly by case id, because no transaction ever spans two cases. So the write authority scales by adding partitions, and I'd key on case id from day one so the split is possible later. The one hot shape is a single case with thousands of events, which policy should cap, and the snapshot means reads never replay it.

The deadline sweeper's query is the thing I'd check first for performance: status equals open and due before now, on millions of rows. Index on status and due time, bounded batches, skip-locked claims, and let the timer take the normal path so the sweeper only sees stragglers.

What does not scale by adding machines: Operations. If ten times the cases means ten times the manual reviews, that's the real ceiling. So the honest performance lever at scale is automation, and I'd decide what to automate from shadow results, precision and recall by reason and market, not from average handle time.

Two things I'd deliberately not add, because the numbers don't ask for them: a queue between the API and the case store to absorb spikes, which at eight writes a second is a component with no job and one more place to lose a command; and sharding for throughput, when the reason to partition here is failover size, not writes. I'd rather say that than draw them.

And on latency specifically: the customer-facing path is one indexed read plus one commit. Policy evaluation is precomputed at case open. If p99 is bad, it's a hot partition, a lock held across a slow call, or a synchronous call that shouldn't be there, in that order. I would not cache the case; a cached case is exactly what lets two operators decide it twice.

Whiteboard, minute 36 (added to the side)

The interviewer makes a suggestion. The prep document says they welcome pushback; silence here reads as passivity.

Interviewer

I'd actually push you to cache the case. Ops reads are most of the traffic, and a cache in front of the case store is the standard move.

Candidate

I'd push back on that one, and let me say why rather than just no. The thing Ops reads a case for is to decide on it, and a cached case is a stale decision input: two operators can each read the cached version, each believe the case is undecided, and the version check only catches the second one after they've both done the work. That's the double-decision bug with the latency hidden.

If the goal is read latency, I'd get it a different way. Ops discovery already comes from the search projection, and the case read itself is one indexed row from the snapshot, which I can serve from a synchronous replica. What I'd happily cache is the versioned policy and reference data, because a version is a safe cache key. So: cache the inputs that don't change under you, never the thing you're about to decide on. If you're seeing a read problem I'm not, I'd want to look at the numbers before choosing.

Interviewer

Fair. Keep going.

36:00

Deep dive two: failures as a quality, six questions per failure. The candidate named this dive at minute 18; the interviewer runs it.

Interviewer

Let's break things. The card network times out on a submission.

Candidate

Same discipline as the ledger: unknown, not failed. The network may have processed it before the response was lost. I query by the same external reference. If they support idempotent resubmission, I retry with that reference; if they don't, I escalate before I create another submission. Every attempt and response is persisted, because if this becomes a deadline dispute, the record of what we sent when is our evidence. Customer sees "sent to the network, awaiting response". What I never do is resubmit with a new reference; that can open a second dispute.

Interviewer

Your timer workers are down for an hour, over a batch of deadlines.

Candidate

The deadlines are rows, so nothing is lost. When workers return, or the sweeper runs, they query due and near-due rows, claim each conditionally with a lease, process idempotently, and escalate anything already breached. I measure oldest unprocessed deadline and time-to-breach, and I alert before breach, not after. If workers are down long enough that a statutory deadline is at risk, that's a page to Operations with the list, so a human can act out of cycle.

Interviewer

Two operators decide the same case at the same second. One says won, one says lost.

Candidate

Both commands carry the expected case version. The first commits and bumps the version. The second's conditional update matches zero rows and gets a conflict. It reloads, sees the case is decided, and is rejected, or the operator consciously retries with a reason if policy allows a re-decision. The audit trail shows both attempts and one decision. Never last-write-wins.

Interviewer

Point at your diagram. What's the single point of failure, and what does a customer see when it goes?

Candidate

The case database's primary. Everything else is either stateless behind a load balancer, workers and adapters and API instances, or a rebuildable projection. For the primary: a synchronous replica in another zone, so failover loses nothing; automated promotion, which takes seconds to a minute; and during that window commands return "try again" while reads keep working from the snapshot replica. The customer sees a brief retry message on a submit, nothing on a read.

And the rest degrades rather than dies. Network down: cases still open, submissions queue with stable references, customer sees "sent, awaiting". Ledger down: decisions continue, credits pend with their keys, customer sees "approved, being applied". Storage down: uploads refused with a retry message, nothing else affected. Search down: Ops works from queues built off the case store. Kafka down: commands unaffected, outbox backlog grows, notifications late.

Interviewer

Different kind of failure. An operator credits a friend's account through a made-up dispute.

Candidate

Three layers, because one won't do. Commands are role-based, with four-eyes approval above an amount threshold and per-role limits, so one person can't take a large case from open to credited alone. Every command records actor and reason in the event history, so it's discoverable, and I'd add a control that flags operators deciding cases for accounts linked to them. And the ledger independently restricts which command types it accepts from this platform and at what limits, so even an engineer with database access can't move money by editing a row here. Friendly fraud from the customer side is the same family: business uniqueness, velocity limits per customer, and provisional-credit caps by risk band, which is a policy lever I'd name to Risk rather than decide myself.

Interviewer

A policy release goes out wrong and misclassifies five thousand cases before anyone notices.

Candidate

Freeze that policy version for new evaluations first. Then find affected cases by version, which I can do precisely because every decision recorded its version. Classify impact: customer harm, money moved, deadlines affected. Run an approved remediation command that appends corrective events and any required financial actions, case by case, with the reason. Notify customers and regulators as required. Reconcile every corrected case. Then the retrospective: why tests and rollout gates didn't catch it. What I don't do is rewrite the event history or bulk-edit statuses; the wrong decision happened and the record must say so.

Whiteboard, minute 42

43:00

Fourth interruption: the prompt shape changes.

Interviewer

Now suppose the network isn't an API. We send a CSV by FTP four times a day and get a file back. What changes?

Candidate

The case model doesn't change. NetworkSubmission still has a stable reference; that reference becomes the row id in the file. What I add is a BatchRun, one file, one upload, one status, with an id from the date and the slot, and a reconciliation queue for rows sent but not yet answered.

Outbound: at 14:00, in one transaction, claim every queued submission into run 2026-09-06-14 with a conditional update, so a duplicate scheduler claims nothing. Build the CSV deterministically from the claimed rows, checksum it, upload it under a name derived from the run id, mark the run uploaded. Every row is now awaiting answer.

Inbound: download the response file, checksum it, dedupe by file id so a file delivered twice is ignored, parse row by row, quarantine bad rows without dropping the file, and turn each good row into a case command keyed by file id plus row id. So a half-processed file can be re-run safely.

Failures change shape but not discipline. Upload timeout is unknown: check the remote directory for the run's file and checksum before re-uploading the same file under the same name; never rebuild with a new run id, that resends two hundred disputes. No response for two days: our cases are fine; the reconciliation queue ages, and its oldest item is what pages someone, plus escalation for any case near its deadline.

Whiteboard, minute 45

45:00

The last dive, short: rollout and ownership in three minutes, because the two chosen dives took the depth.

Interviewer

Last few minutes. How do you get this to production, and who owns what?

Candidate

Narrowly. One country, one network, one reason-code family, chosen with business data for low exposure and a cooperative Ops owner, not the biggest market. First, shadow the eligibility and deadline maths: feed production inputs to the new engine with every side effect suppressed, store its outputs separately, compare to the trusted process. Shadow must be physically unable to emit network or ledger commands. Then enable network submissions behind a kill switch, then money actions behind their own kill switch. A kill switch stops new side effects but keeps committed intents visible and lets safe acknowledgements drain; it never kills workers and loses track of what was promised.

Ownership: the platform team owns case mechanics, transitions, audit, deadlines, idempotency. Legal and compliance own what the rules mean, versioned and approved. Operations owns queues and the exception procedures. Network teams own adapters and certification. The ledger team owns money and independently enforces which commands it accepts. Product owns what the customer reads and when they see money.

For Colombia: centralise the mechanics and a governed policy framework; the Colombia owner publishes a versioned rule package under common contracts and tests. No fork, and no forcing every local rule into global code.

The trade-off I'd flag as biggest: automation versus human review. I'd automate only what shadow results prove we can measure, gradually, with thresholds and easy escalation.

Whiteboard, minute 49

50:00

Interviewer

If you had to name the single most important decision in this design?

Candidate

That a case command commits workflow state and intent locally, in one transaction, and every external side effect happens after commit and comes back as an event. Everything else follows: audit is free because history is events; deadlines can't vanish because they're rows in that commit; money is safe because the intent is committed with its key before anyone calls the ledger; and "won" and "financially settled" stay separate because one is a decision we record and the other is a fact the ledger reports back.

Interviewer

Thanks. Any questions for me?

Candidate

Five places this candidate could still lose points

  • Drawing the rung below the floor. The prep document wants a first idea and its flaws; the video says a design that cannot serve the features would not pass at any level, and that a senior candidate starts further on. Name the one-table version in one sentence, with its four flaws; that is the collaboration signal, and it takes forty seconds. Drawing it costs five minutes of deep dive.
  • Reaching the drawing late. The video's pace for senior and staff candidates is the high-level design by about minute 10 of your own time, minute 15 on Nubank's clock. If the qualities run long, say the invariants as a list, not a speech.
  • Talking through the diagram instead of drawing it. The architecture must be on the board by minute 16. If you're narrating boxes that aren't drawn, the interviewer can't check your write path.
  • Over-answering the interruptions. Each one above is under 90 seconds. If the tenfold answer runs five minutes, you never reach rollout, and rollout is where "has shipped things" is graded.
  • Forgetting to ask the interviewer questions at the end. Two good ones, tied to the design, are cheap and remembered.

The bar for a Lead, in the video's terms

The video grades three levels and says where each passes. Mid-level: breadth over depth, the batch solution, probing on the basics; the interviewer drives the second half. Senior: reaches the high-level design fast, contrasts the options and chooses, goes deep on two or three of the dives with the trade-offs said; drives, and anticipates problems. Staff: breezes through the basics, drives every chosen dive to the bottom, "the interviewer should intervene only to focus, not to steer", and ideally teaches the interviewer something. The Lead round sits between senior and staff, and the script above is written to that: two dives to the bottom (provisional credit, failures), one dive in three minutes (rollout), the batch-file answer at 43 as the teach-the-interviewer moment, and a pushback with a reason each time the interviewer suggests a component the numbers don't ask for. The sentence the video's interviewer uses for the last of those: "identifications that go against the grain are really effective ways to show seniority."
