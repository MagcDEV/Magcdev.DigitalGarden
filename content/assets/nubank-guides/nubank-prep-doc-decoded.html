They sent you the rubric. This guide explains every concept it names in plain terms, shows how each one already lives in your chargeback and ledger designs, and adds the two things those designs didn't stress: rough numbers, and a v1-then-iterate storyline.

1. The end result: what this document changes

Read this first; it re-weights your last six days.

The round is 60 minutes, you lead, and the conversation will "center on distributed systems, scalability, performance, and efficiency". They will grade whether you narrow the problem before drawing, whether you use rough numbers to decide, whether you start simple and iterate, and what happens on a bad day. Your chargeback design already does most of this. What it must add is arithmetic and a visible v1.

Five things the PDF says that the candidate reports didn't

  1. The example prompts are generic. The PDF's worked example is ride-hailing and its recommended video is an ad-click aggregator. That doesn't mean your prompt won't be fintech (three 2026 candidates got chargebacks), but it means the interviewers think in general distributed-systems terms and might hand you a non-banking problem. Section 8 gives you two ten-minute sketches as insurance.
  2. Numbers are expected, and they must lead somewhere. "Precision isn't the goal; clear mathematical reasoning is." Your guides reason from invariants; they rarely say "that's 8 writes per second". Section 4 fixes that.
  3. "Drawing an architecture in the first three minutes is the most common way a strong engineer scores poorly." Your stage 1 (clarify) is exactly right. Spend the full five to eight minutes there and say the scoping decision out loud.
  4. Begin simple, then iterate. They want to hear "my first idea is X, the pros are, the cons are, we tackle that next by". Your guides present the finished design. Section 6 turns it into a v1 → v2 → v3 story.
  5. It's a two-way discussion. They welcome pushback on their suggestions. When the interviewer proposes something (a cache, a workflow engine), disagree politely with a reason if you have one. Silence reads as passivity.

4. Numbers: the part you were missing

"How do you support architectural decisions with numbers (volume, latency, throughput), even as rough estimates?" Here is how, for your two designs and for any prompt.

The arithmetic you should have memorised

FactUse
A day is 86,400 seconds; call it 100,000Per-day volume ÷ 100k = per second. 8.6 million a day ≈ 100 per second.
A month ≈ 2.6 million seconds; a year ≈ 30 millionStorage growth: events per month × bytes per event.
Peak ≈ 3 to 10× averageDesign for peak; say which multiplier you chose and why (payday, Black Friday).
1 KB ≈ a small JSON event; 1 MB ≈ a photo; 10 MB ≈ a PDF with scansStorage per case, per transaction.
One Postgres node: thousands of simple writes per second, tens of thousands of indexed reads, comfortable up to a few TBWhether one write authority is enough.
One Kafka partition: tens of thousands of small messages per secondWhether the bus is ever the bottleneck (usually not).

A human reviews maybe 30 to 60 cases a dayThe Operations ceiling, in people.

Chargebacks, worked out loud

Say the assumptions, do the maths on the board, land on a decision.

Assume 100M customers, ~2B card transactions / month
Dispute rate ~0.1%                → 2M disputes / month  ≈ 70k / day  ≈ 0.8 / s average, ~8 / s peak
Events per case ~20 × 1 KB        → 40M events / month  ≈ 40 GB / month  ≈ 0.5 TB / year
Evidence: 2 files × 2 MB per case → 8 TB / month         (object storage, not the database)
Deadlines: ~3 per case            → 6M rows / month; sweeper scans the OPEN ones only
Batch file: 70k rows / 4 slots    → ~18k rows per file; a few MB; trivial to build, painful to resend
Ops: 30% need a human, 40 / day   → 2M × 0.3 / 30 days / 40 ≈ 500 reviewers

The decision the numbers force: under one write per second average and tens at peak, a single well-indexed relational database is not the bottleneck and won't be at 10×. Partition by case id for isolation and failover size, not for throughput. The costs that scale badly are evidence storage (cheap, but plan retention) and human review (expensive; automation is the real lever). So the design spends its complexity on correctness, deadlines and reconciliation, and on measuring what to automate.

The ledger, worked out loud

2B transactions / month ≈ 770 / s average, ~8k / s peak (Black Friday, payday)
2 entries per transaction + 1 aggregate update + 1 outbox row ≈ 4 writes  → ~30k writes / s peak
Entries 200 bytes × 4B / month ≈ 0.8 TB / month on the hot path
Read: balance checks ≈ same as transactions; feed reads 10× that, served from projections

The decision: 30k writes per second at peak is beyond one comfortable Postgres primary, so partitioning by customer is on the roadmap from day one, and the failover time of each partition is the availability ceiling. Cross-customer transfers then need the clearing account. Analytics never touches the transactional store.

The authoriser, one line

Budget: the network expects an answer in a few hundred ms; a cross-region hop is 100+ → the decision must be local.
One indexed read + one small commit ≈ 5–10 ms → fits with room; a synchronous fraud model call must fit in ~50 ms or be shed.

The pattern for any prompt

  1. Users or entities → actions per user per day → total per day → ÷ 100k → per second → × peak factor.
  2. Bytes per action → per day → per month → per year. Compare with what one machine holds in memory and on disk.
  3. Latency budget → what a hop costs → what can be synchronous.
  4. End with the sentence "so that's why I…": partition, cache, go async, precompute, or, just as validly, don't.

Avoid

"It'll be a lot of data, so we need something scalable." And the opposite error: over-engineering a system whose numbers say one database is fine. Saying "at this volume one Postgres is enough, and here's the number that would change my mind" is a strong answer.

5. Every technical concept they list, from zero

Their "Technical Concepts to Review", each explained plainly, then how it shows up in an answer.

Distributed systems fundamentals

Synchronous vs asynchronousWhat: synchronous means the caller waits for the result; asynchronous means the caller gets "accepted" and the work finishes later. When each fits: synchronous for anything the caller needs before proceeding and that is fast and local (the atomic commit). Asynchronous for anything slow, external, or failure-prone (the card network, the ledger call, sending a notification). In your answer: "acceptance is synchronous; every external step is asynchronous with an explicit Processing state." The trade: async is more resilient and faster to answer, but you now need state for "in flight" and a way to observe results.
Batch vs streamWhat: batch processes many items together on a schedule (the 14:00 CSV); stream processes each item as it arrives (Kafka events into the feed). Trade-offs: batch is simpler, easier to reconcile as a unit, cheap per item, but latency is the schedule and a bad file blocks many items. Stream is low latency and isolates failures per item, but ordering, duplicates and backpressure become your problem. In your answer: the network leg is batch because the counterparty said so; everything internal is stream; and you can name the moment they meet: "one command per row, keyed by file plus row".
Consistency vs availabilityWhat: when the network between machines is partitioned, you can either keep answering (available) or refuse until you're sure you have the latest data (consistent), not both. This is the CAP trade-off. In finance: for anything that decides about money you choose consistent: decline rather than approve from a possibly stale balance. For anything you only read, choose available: show a slightly stale feed. In your answer: draw the line and name it: "case transitions, deadlines, financial intent: strong. Search, notifications, dashboards: eventual. Uploaded-but-unanswered: explicitly unknown."
Idempotency and retries; at-least-onceWhat: networks lose responses, so callers retry, so receivers see duplicates. "At-least-once delivery" is the honest guarantee: every message arrives, some twice. Idempotency is the property that a duplicate has no extra effect. You get it with a stable key chosen where the intent is born, stored atomically with the effect. How it changes a design: every boundary gets a key; every consumer dedupes; timeouts mean unknown, and you query before you retry with the same key. In your answer: name the key on every arrow, unprompted.

Data and storage

Choosing a store for the access patternRelational (Postgres, MySQL): tables, transactions across rows, unique constraints, rich queries. Choose when you need atomic multi-row writes and constraints, which is every "decide" path in finance. Key-value (Redis, DynamoDB): one key → one blob, extremely fast, no joins. Choose for lookups by id at scale: idempotency records, session state, hot counters. Document (MongoDB, DynamoDB again): flexible nested records, queried by fields. Choose for varied shapes you read whole: a case snapshot with nested evidence metadata could live here. Columnar (BigQuery, Redshift, ClickHouse): stores columns together for fast scans over billions of rows. Choose for analytics, never for the transactional path. In your answer: "relational for the case store because I need multi-row transactions and unique keys; object storage for bytes; a search index for Ops; columnar for reporting fed from events." And: "I'd stay relational for the aggregate; the numbers don't justify anything else."
Modeling around the queriesWhat: design tables for the questions you'll ask, not just for the things that exist. Example: "find deadlines due in the next hour that nobody has claimed" needs an index on (status, due_at). "Show Ops all cases in Mexico over 20 days old" needs a projection, because the case table isn't shaped for it. In your answer: list the three or four queries the system lives on, and put an index or a projection next to each.
Partitioning and shardingWhat: splitting data across several databases by a key so each holds and serves a share. Partitioning is the general idea; sharding usually means doing it across separate servers. The key decides everything: queries within one key are cheap; queries across keys are expensive; transactions across keys are impossible without a saga. In your answer: "case id, because no transaction spans two cases" (chargebacks) or "customer id, because customer-to-customer interaction is low, and then cross-customer transfers go through clearing" (ledger). Always name the hot-key risk.
Schema evolutionWhat: the shape of your data will change after launch; how do you change it without downtime or breaking old records? Rules: add fields, never repurpose them; make new fields optional with a default; write code that reads both old and new shapes for a while (expand, migrate, contract); version event types rather than editing old events; never rewrite history to fit a new schema. In your answer: "events carry a schema version; consumers accept versions N and N+1 during a rollout; the case snapshot can be rebuilt if its shape changes." This is a question interviewers use to separate people who've run systems from people who've drawn them.

Scale and performance

Horizontal vs vertical scaling, load balancingVertical: a bigger machine. Simple, has a ceiling, one failure takes everything. Horizontal: more machines sharing the work. No ceiling in principle, but only for work that can be split: stateless services and idempotent workers scale horizontally; a single write authority doesn't without partitioning. Load balancer: the thing in front of many identical instances that spreads requests across them and stops sending to a dead one. In your answer: "workers, adapters, API instances behind a load balancer: horizontal. The case database: vertical first (the numbers allow it), partitioned by case id when failover size or isolation demands it."
CachingWhat: keeping a fast copy of data near where it's read. What to cache: things read far more than written and tolerant of being slightly stale: policy rules (versioned, so they're safe), reference data, a customer's rendered feed. Where: in the service's memory (fastest, per instance), in a shared cache like Redis (consistent across instances), at the edge (static content). Invalidation: the hard part. Time-based expiry (simple, stale for up to TTL), event-based (invalidate when the source changes; precise, more moving parts), or versioned keys (cache under "policy v12", so a new version is simply a new key). In your answer: "I cache policy by version and reference data with a TTL. I never cache a case for deciding, or a balance for authorising: a cached decision input is a stale decision input, and that's the double-decision bug." Saying what you won't cache is the strong part.
Where the bottleneck movesWhat: fixing the first bottleneck reveals the next. Add workers → the database becomes the limit. Partition the database → cross-partition operations become the limit. Automate review → the model's accuracy becomes the limit. In your answer: after any scaling change, say where the pressure goes next: "once the sweeper query is indexed, the next limit is Ops throughput, and after that, evidence scanning."

Reliability and operations

Replication and failover; the single point of failureReplication: keeping copies of the database on other machines. Synchronous replication waits for the copy to confirm before committing (no data loss, higher latency); asynchronous doesn't (fast, may lose the last moments on failover). Failover: promoting a copy to primary when the primary dies; takes seconds to a minute; during it, writes fail. SPOF: the component whose failure stops everything. In your answer: point at your own diagram: "my single point of failure is the case database's primary. Synchronous replica in another zone, automated failover, and during the failover window the API returns 'try again' for commands while reads keep working from the snapshot replica." For money: "I decline during failover rather than approve from a replica."
Graceful degradationWhat: when a dependency is down, the product gets worse in a controlled way instead of failing entirely. In your answer: a matrix (section 7): network down → cases still open, submissions queue, customer sees "sent, awaiting"; ledger down → case decisions continue, credits pend, customer told; search down → Ops works from a case-id list; storage down → uploads refused with a retry message, nothing else affected.
Monitoring and debuggingMeasure: queue age per dependency, p99 of the command path, oldest unprocessed deadline, outbox lag, error rate per dependency, reconciliation exceptions, fallback rate. Alert on: thresholds that map to customer or legal impact (time-to-breach), not on raw error counts. Debug: a correlation id on every command that follows it through every event, message and external call, so one id gives you the whole story of one case; structured logs keyed by case id; the event history itself as the primary debugging tool ("what happened to CB-812?" is a query). In your answer: "one correlation id per command, present in every log line and event; when something's wrong I read the case's events first, then the dependency dashboards."
Rolling out safely, rolling backWhat: turning a change on gradually (a cohort, a country, a percentage), watching metrics, and turning it off fast if they move. Rules: code rolls back; data doesn't. Deploy schema changes before the code that needs them. Feature flags and kill switches separate "deployed" from "enabled". Shadow first for anything that decides. In your answer: your stage 7, with the words "and the rollback for a bad policy version is freeze plus fix-forward, because history is immutable".

6. Begin simple, then iterate: chargebacks as v1 → v2 → v3

They want to see you start with a v1, find its flaws yourself, and fix them in order. Here is the storyline; the end state is the design you already know.

v1: the honest first sketch (minute 10)

Say the pros: one service, one database, easy to build, easy to understand, fine at 0.8 cases per second.

Then find the flaws yourself, in this order:

  1. A status column can't answer "who moved this case and why", so audit is a separate log that can disagree with the truth.
  2. Synchronous network and ledger calls hold a database transaction open across a slow external hop; a timeout leaves the case in a state that doesn't match reality, and a retry can credit twice.
  3. The cron job is the only guardian of legal deadlines; if it dies, deadlines die with it.
  4. Two operators updating the same row: last write wins, silently.

v2: fix correctness (minutes 12–20)

What each change fixes: events plus snapshot fix flaw 1; commit-then-side-effects with keys fix flaw 2; deadline rows with a sweeper fix flaw 3; the expected version fixes flaw 4. What it costs: two writes per command instead of one, a worker fleet, and explicit "in flight" states the product must display. Say the cost.

v3: scale and operate (only when the numbers or the interviewer ask)

  • Partition the case store by case id when failover size or isolation demands it; the numbers don't demand it for throughput.
  • Move Ops discovery fully to projections; add the columnar store for reporting.
  • The batch-file variant for networks that require it: batch runs, per-row keys, reconciliation queue.
  • Automation of review from shadow results, with kill switches per side effect.

The words that make this a collaboration signal

"My first idea is one service and one table with a status column. That's fine at our volume, but it has four problems I'd hit in the first month. Let me fix them in the order they'd hurt."

Monitoring and debugging in one breath

"One correlation id per command, on every log line, event and external call. I measure queue age per dependency, p99 of the command path, oldest unprocessed deadline and time-to-breach, outbox lag, reconciliation exceptions, and quarantine counts. 8. If the prompt is generic: two ten-minute sketches

The PDF's own examples. Enough to open confidently; the seven stages carry the rest.

Ride-hailing: match riders with nearby drivers

Requirements to ask (theirs, verbatim in spirit): active riders and drivers at peak, in how many cities; location freshness (every second? five?); "nearby" as distance or ETA; can two riders get the same driver; latency budget for a match; what happens when a driver stops reporting; what's out of scope (pricing, payments, routing).

Numbers: say 1M active drivers reporting every 3 s → ~330k location updates/s, 50 bytes each → 16 MB/s, ~1.4 TB/day. Far past one host's memory, so partition location state by geographic cell.

Scope sentence: "I'll focus on location ingestion and matching, since that's the hard part at this scale."

v1: drivers post location to a service that writes a key-value store keyed by driver; a rider request scans drivers in the city. Flaw: scanning a city per request doesn't scale. v2: index drivers by geo-cell (geohash or H3); a match queries the rider's cell and neighbours; location updates stream through Kafka partitioned by cell; matching services own cells. The invariant: one driver, one active match: a conditional claim on the driver record (a version, like your case version) so two riders can't both win. Failures: driver in a tunnel → last-known location with a staleness stamp, excluded after N seconds; matcher host dies → cells reassigned, in-flight matches retried with the same request key; hot cell (a stadium) → split the cell or add replicas for reads. Consistency line: the claim is strong; the map of nearby cars is eventual.

Ad-click aggregator: count clicks per ad in near real time

Requirements: clicks per second at peak; how fresh must the counts be (seconds? minutes?); exactly how accurate (billing-grade or dashboard-grade); query shapes (per ad per minute, top ads); retention; out of scope (fraud detection? attribution?).

Numbers: 10k clicks/s average, 100k peak, 100 bytes → 10 MB/s peak, ~100 GB/day raw. Aggregated per ad per minute it's tiny.

v1: write every click to a database and run SUM queries. Flaw: 100k inserts/s and expensive queries. v2: clicks go to Kafka partitioned by ad id; stream processors keep per-ad-per-minute counters in memory with checkpoints, flush to a store built for time-series reads; raw clicks also land in cheap storage for reconciliation and replay. The trade: stream gives freshness; batch over the raw log gives exactness; run both and reconcile (the lambda idea), or accept at-least-once with idempotent click ids for near-exact. Failures: processor dies → replay from checkpoint, duplicates deduped by click id within a window; hot ad → split its partition or pre-aggregate at the edge; late clicks → windows stay open for a grace period, then corrections are appended, never edited (immutability again). Consistency line: the dashboard is eventual; the billing number comes from the batch reconciliation.

Notice how much is the same as your fintech designs: a stable key per unit of work, a conditional claim for the one thing that must be exclusive, strong where you decide and eventual where you read, replay from an immutable log, corrections appended not edited. Say that resemblance out loud; it's the "architecture fundamentals" signal.

Two more if you have time

  • 10 years of engineering at Nubank: sharding introduced in 2016 worked for a time but eventually hit physical limits, including AWS running out of machines to match their pace. Good for "where the bottleneck moves": even sharding has a ceiling.
  • Inside Nubank's engineering: immutability as the organising principle: Datomic, Kafka for asynchronous communication, functional Clojure, chosen to minimise state changes and make problems easier to find and fix. This is the "architecture fundamentals" signal in their own words.
  • Lucas Cavalcanti, "Architecting Software for Leverage" (InfoQ talk on Nubank's architectural decisions): buy-versus-build at the start (they integrated a card processor rather than building one), the vendor not scaling, running several copies of the whole system, hexagonal architecture, Kafka as a persistent log with a TTL. Pragmatic-thinking material.

The Datomic trade-off that explains everything

One page: docs.datomic.com/datomic-overview. Datomic is developed and maintained by Nubank and used in billions of their transactions a day. The overview states its two defining properties plainly:

  • Trivial read scaling: because databases are immutable, any number of processes can hold copies to serve reads.
  • No per-database write scaling: transactions are totally serialized; only one transaction happens at a time in a given database.

Hold those two facts together and Nubank's whole architecture falls out. Immutability gives them audit for free and reads that scale by copying. Serialized writes mean one database can't absorb the whole bank, so they shard by customer (and, per the cloud-limits post, shard everything else too). Sharding by customer makes cross-customer movements the hard problem, which is your clearing-account section. In the scaling stage, one sentence does the work:

Say it once, at the scaling stage

"Your own database makes this trade explicit: Datomic gives you immutability and free read scaling in exchange for serialized writes per database, which is exactly why you shard by customer, and exactly why cross-shard movement is the part I'd spend the most care on."

The video they recommended

Hello Interview, "Design an Ad Click Aggregator" (the written breakdown is on the same page). Watch it once for the habits, not the answer:

  • It splits requirements into core ones and "below the line (out of scope)". Use that phrase when you scope; they chose to endorse it.
  • It asks the interviewer about scale before writing non-functional requirements, then commits to numbers: 10M active ads, a peak of 10k clicks per second, about 100M clicks a day.
  • Its non-functional list is a good template: scalable to the stated peak, low-latency queries, fault-tolerant and accurate collection (no lost clicks), as real-time as possible, and idempotent tracking so the same click isn't counted twice.
  • For data-processing problems it defines the system's interface (input: click data; output: metrics) and the data flow before the high-level design. If you get a pipeline-shaped prompt, do the same.

Their other recommendation is Alex Xu's System Design Interview. If you only open one part, open the back-of-envelope estimation chapter and, in volume 2, the payment system and ad-click aggregation chapters; those three overlap with sections 4 and 8 of this guide.

12. Six more edges

Small investments with outsized return, given six days.

  1. Two stories from your own work. Their PDF says to revisit design decisions from past projects because your experience can lead to a stronger solution. Pick two from your banking years and rehearse each in ninety seconds: what you chose, what you gave up, what broke later. Best shapes: a time you handled duplicates or idempotency, and a production failure you found and fixed. When they ask "have you dealt with this?", answer with a real one.
  2. One mock with a human, on the real tool. The PDF calls talking through a design out loud the most valuable preparation you can do. You've read and written; the gap is speaking while drawing. Have someone read the interviewer turns from the mock-interview file, inject interruptions at random, in Excalidraw or Miro, camera on. Record it and watch ten minutes back; you'll hear the filler and the silences.
  3. Practise disagreeing politely. They welcome pushback. Have your mock partner propose "let's cache the case" and "let's use Kafka as the store"; answer with the reason, then offer the alternative. One rehearsed disagreement makes the real one natural.
  4. A paper card beside you. The arithmetic table from section 4, your five clarifying questions, the scope sentence, and the six failure questions. Nobody minds, and it removes the one thing that goes first under pressure: numbers.
  5. Draw the diagram in under three minutes. Boxes, labelled arrows with the key each carries, nothing else. A clear board is on their list; a slow board eats the deep dive.
  6. Setup on Sunday. Quiet room, stable connection, camera, mic, the whiteboard tool open in a test board. It's the last item on their checklist and the cheapest to get right.
