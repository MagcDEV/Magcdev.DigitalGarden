Chargebacks on one card

The chargeback guide is 15 sections. This is the part worth memorising, in plain words, in the order you will say it. If you can say everything on this page without looking, you can pass the round.

Condensed 1 October 2026 from The chargeback platform, end to end and the mock interview. Short on purpose: when something here feels thin, that is the section of the big guide to open.

1. The one sentence

A chargeback platform is a system that manages a case over weeks. It owns the story of the dispute: what happened, who did it, when, and under which rule. It asks other systems to do things: the card network to carry the dispute, storage to hold documents, the ledger to move money. It is not the ledger and it is not the network.

What it must never do: lose a deadline, forget a side effect it promised, lose the audit trail, or confuse "the case is won" with "the customer has been paid".

The sentence that ties everything together

"A case command commits workflow state and intent locally; external side effects happen after commit and come back as new events. Won is a decision; financially settled is a fact."

3. Step 1: questions, numbers, rules

Five questions, before anything

  1. Are we the issuer (the customer's bank) or the acquirer (the merchant's bank)? Assume issuer.
  2. Which countries and networks on day one? Each changes rules and deadlines.
  3. Who owns the rules and deadlines? Legal does. We implement them as versioned policy.
  4. Does opening a case move money? No. Provisional credit is a separate decision.
  5. What already exists? A ledger, a card transaction store, identity, storage, notifications. We integrate, we don't rebuild.

Plus one shape question: is the network a real-time API or files by FTP? Design for the API. Say how a file changes it, as in section 10.

The features (what users can do)

A customer disputes a transaction and sees its status. An operator reviews evidence and decides. The platform submits to the network and records the outcome. The platform asks the ledger for credit, and for its reversal. Below the line: fraud scoring, the merchant side, reporting.

The numbers (ask for them first, then do the maths)

100M customers, 2B card transactions a month, 0.1% disputed
→ 2M cases a month ≈ 70k a day ≈ under 1 per second, ~8 at peak
20 events per case × 1 KB → 40 GB a month        (small)
2 files × 2 MB per case  → 8 TB a month          (object storage, not the DB)
30% need a human, 40 cases a day each → ~500 reviewers

The decision the numbers force

"Seventy thousand a day is under one write a second. One good relational database handles it, and would at ten times. This is not a throughput problem. It's a correctness, deadline and people problem, and that's where I'll spend the design."

The seven rules (the qualities, as rules)

  1. A case only moves through valid transitions, under a versioned policy.
  2. Every action records who, why, when. No anonymous status change.
  3. A deadline is a saved row. It cannot disappear if every worker dies.
  4. Every money action has its own key. A retry never credits twice.
  5. Every message to the network reuses the same reference. A new one could open a second dispute.
  6. Evidence is versioned and access-controlled. Old bytes are never overwritten.
  7. Every decision records the policy version that made it.

The scope sentence, at minute twelve

"I'll scope to the issuer-side workflow with provisional credit, because that's where correctness is hardest. The network is an adapter, the ledger is an existing system. Speed matters less than in payments; audit, deadlines and money correctness are strict."

4. Step 2: six nouns, three operations

DisputeCaseThe current summary: state, amount, country, network, owner, policy version. The row you read.
CaseEventOne fact in its history: opened, evidence added, submitted, won. Never edited. The snapshot can be rebuilt from these.
DeadlineA row with a due time, a type and a status. Not a timer in memory.
EvidenceItemMetadata about a document: where the bytes are, checksum, scan status, version. The bytes live in object storage.
NetworkSubmissionThe stable reference we sent to the network, and whether they acknowledged it.
FinancialActionOne money step with its own ledger key, like CB-812:PROVISIONAL_CREDIT:v1. We record the intent here. The ledger posts the money.

Three operations. Open a case (with an idempotency key from the app). Run a command on a case (type, expected version, reason, key). Read a case (snapshot, or history as of a date). Everything is a command on the case. Nobody edits a status column.

5. Step 3: the drawing

The version you name and don't draw: "One service, one table with a status column, synchronous calls to the network and the ledger, a cron for deadlines. It works for a week, then it fails audit, retries and deadlines. So I won't draw it."

The version you draw. A command comes in. Four things happen in order:

  1. Check: who is calling, the idempotency key, the expected case version.
  2. Validate the transition against the current policy version.
  3. One commit writes: the new event, the updated snapshot and version, any deadlines, the idempotency record, and outbox rows for every side effect.
  4. Return the new state by name: "submitted", "credit pending".

After the commit, workers read the outbox and call the network, the ledger, storage, notifications. Whatever they see comes back into the case as a new event: "network acked", "ledger posted tx 9182". So the case's own history says what happened outside it.

Two more boxes. A timer wakes workers near a due time. A sweeper reads the deadline rows every few minutes and catches what the timer missed. A search index built from events, for Ops to find cases. It may lag, so Ops opens the real case by id before acting.

The key on every arrow. App → platform: the request key. Platform → ledger: the action key. Platform → network: the submission reference. File row: file id plus row id.

Say its cost, then its gaps

"It costs two writes per command instead of one, a worker fleet, and in-flight states the product has to show. I'd go deepest on provisional credit and failures."

The consistency line, said once: strong where I decide (transitions, deadlines, money intent); eventual where I only read (search, notifications, dashboards); explicitly unknown where the world hasn't answered yet.

6. One case, end to end, on the diagram

Ana disputes a R$300 purchase at a petrol station in Brazil, on a Visa card. Follow the numbers.

  1. Ana taps "I don't recognise this purchase". Her app creates a request key, K1, once for this tap, and sends an OpenDispute command with it. If the network is slow and the app retries, it sends the same K1.
  2. The case service checks, then validates. Is this really Ana? Has K1 been seen before (if yes, return the same case and stop)? What case version does the command expect (none, it's new)? Then, under policy version 12 for Brazil and Visa: Ana owns the card, there is no open case for this transaction, and the purchase is inside the dispute window.
  3. One commit writes five things together. A CaseEvent OPENED (actor Ana, time, policy v12). The DisputeCase snapshot (state OPENED, version 1). A Deadline row: Ana must supply evidence within 10 days. The record of K1. And outbox rows: notify Ana, and evaluate provisional credit. All or nothing.
  4. The service answers "case CB-812 opened". Ana's app shows "under review". Nothing outside the platform has been called yet. That is on purpose. The promise to call them is already saved, in the outbox rows.
  5. After the commit, workers read the outbox. The credit worker evaluates policy: Ana is owed provisional credit. It sends a RequestProvisionalCredit command, which goes through boxes 2 and 3 again. That commit writes a FinancialAction with key CB-812:PROVISIONAL_CREDIT:v1 and a new outbox row. The ledger worker picks it up and calls the ledger with exactly that key.
  6. The ledger posts the money. +300 to Ana, −300 to the disputes clearing account, and it returns transaction 9182. If the call times out, the worker retries the same key. The ledger either returns 9182 or posts once now. Never a new key.
  7. What the worker saw comes back as an event. PROVISIONAL_CREDIT_POSTED, tx 9182. Ana sees R$300 in her account. The case still says "under review": won is a decision and paid is a fact, and neither has happened yet.
  8. Ana uploads a screenshot of her chat with the merchant. The app asks for a staged upload. The bytes go straight to encrypted object storage, which returns a token and a checksum. The case row never holds the file.
  9. A command links that exact object version to the case (through box 3 again): an EvidenceItem in state UPLOADED. A scan worker checks checksum, format and malware, then a command moves it to EVIDENCE_READY. Only now is it evidence the platform may send.
  10. Submission to the network. A worker evaluates eligibility under policy v12: ELIGIBLE. A Submit command writes a NetworkSubmission with reference NB-CB-812-1 and an outbox row, and returns "submitted", which means durably intended. The adapter sends it with that reference. The network acknowledges. A worker writes NETWORK_ACKED (step 7 again). In that same commit the evidence deadline is completed and a new Deadline row is created: the merchant has 30 days to respond. Had the adapter's call timed out, it would query the network by NB-CB-812-1 before ever sending again.
  11. Thirty days of waiting. The deadline row sits in the database. A timer wakes a worker near the due time. A sweeper reads due and near-due rows every few minutes, in case the timer died. Alerts fire before breach. In Ana's case nothing fires, because the network answers in time.
  12. Meanwhile, Ops can see it. Every event also updates the search index. So an operator sees CB-812 in the "awaiting network" queue with its age and deadline. If the index lags, Ops still opens the real case by id before acting on it.
  13. Week four: the award. The network returns its decision. A Decide command (boxes 2, 3) writes the event. Won in full: the credit stands and the FinancialAction is finalised. Lost: a new action CB-812:REVERSE_PROVISIONAL:v1 asks the ledger for a linked reversal, through steps 5, 6 and 7 again. Ana may go negative, which is a product decision made before launch. Won R$200 of 300: one adjustment FINAL_ADJUSTMENT:v2 for −100. The case closes only when the decision is recorded and every ledger action is confirmed. The three truths must agree: the network's award, our decision, the ledger's postings. Ana gets a notification. The events tell the whole story to any auditor, as of any date.

The case in one breath

"Ana opens a dispute with a key; one commit saves the event, the snapshot, a deadline and the promises to call outside; she's told 'opened'. Workers then ask the ledger for credit with a key that names that action, and write back what the ledger said. Evidence is bytes in storage until a checked item is committed. Submission reuses one reference. Deadlines are rows a sweeper can always find. Weeks later the award arrives as an event, money moves again under a new key, and the case closes when decision and settlement agree."

7. The money story (Ana's R$300), the three endings

  1. Ana disputes R$300. Policy says she's owed provisional credit. The case commit writes a FinancialAction with key CB-812:PROVISIONAL_CREDIT:v1 and an outbox row. Case state: under review. Money state: credit pending.
  2. After commit, a worker sends that key to the ledger. The ledger posts +300 to Ana, −300 to a disputes clearing account, and returns its transaction id. A new event records it. Ana sees the money.
  3. Weeks later the network answers. Win: the credit stands. Lose: a new action REVERSE_PROVISIONAL:v1 asks the ledger for a linked reversal. Partial win (R$200 of 300): one adjustment FINAL_ADJUSTMENT:v2 for −100. Never edit the credit amount.
  4. The case closes only when the decision is recorded and every ledger action is confirmed. Three truths must agree: the network's award, our decision, the ledger's postings.

Why the key is so specific

"One case legitimately needs several money actions. If the key were the case id, the reversal would look like a retry of the credit and the ledger would refuse it. The key names the business action being retried: this case, this action, this version."

Ledger timeout? "Unknown, not failed. I retry the same key. The ledger returns what it already posted, or posts once now. I never create a new key on a timeout; that's how a customer is credited twice."

8. When things break

For any failure, answer these six: What committed? What retries, with which key? What can duplicate? What does the customer see? Which metric alerts? What does an operator safely do?

FailureAnswer in one line
Network times outUnknown. Query by the same reference before ever resubmitting. Customer sees "sent, awaiting".
Timer workers down an hourDeadlines are rows, nothing lost. The sweeper claims each one with a lease, processes, escalates anything breached. Alert before breach.
Two operators, same case, same secondBoth carry the expected version. One commits; the other gets a conflict, reloads, sees it's decided. Never last-write-wins.
Upload succeeds, commit failsThe file is an orphan, not evidence. A cleanup job deletes it. Bytes existing is not evidence; a committed EvidenceItem that passed its checks is.
Bad policy release misclassifies 5,000 casesFreeze that version, find cases by version, fix forward with new audited events. Never rewrite history.
Operator credits a friendRoles and four-eyes above a threshold; actor on every event; the ledger independently limits what this platform may ask for.
Single point of failure?The case database's primary. Synchronous replica, automated failover in seconds; during it, commands get "try again", reads keep working.

Degrades, never dies. Network down: cases still open, submissions queue. Ledger down: decisions continue, credits pend with their keys. Storage down: uploads refused, nothing else. Search down: Ops works from queues. Kafka down: commands fine, notifications late.

When they say "it's down, now what?"

"Nothing is ever half done, because every side effect is an outbox row written in the same commit. If the network times out, I don't resubmit. I query by the same reference first. If the ledger is down, operators keep deciding and credits pend with their keys. If the timer workers die, deadlines are rows, so the sweeper catches up and we alert before any breach. Each dependency takes down its own feature, never the case."

9. Scale and performance

"Ten times the volume": the five-step answer

  1. Ask what grew. Cases, documents, operators?
  2. Do the maths. 700k cases a day is 8 writes a second, 80 at peak. The database is still fine. Evidence storage becomes a cost. Operations becomes 5,000 people.
  3. Scale what scales: workers, adapters, the search index, storage. All idempotent, all horizontal.
  4. Say what doesn't: Operations. People don't scale by adding machines. The real lever is automation, decided from shadow results.
  5. Protect the rules while scaling. A "performance improvement" that bends one is a bug.

When they suggest caching the case

"I'd push back. Ops reads a case to decide on it, and a cached case is a stale decision input: two operators both read it, both think it's undecided. If it's read latency, I'd serve the snapshot from a replica and cache the versioned policy instead, because a version is a safe cache key."

The follow-up questions, with short answers

A Lead candidate who failed in late 2025 reported mostly scale and performance questions. These are the ones to expect. Answer each with a number, then a decision.

They askAnswer in one line
"Ten times the cases?"700k a day: 8 writes / s, 80 at peak. The database is still fine. Evidence becomes a cost line; Ops becomes 5,000 people.
"Where's your bottleneck?"Operations: people. After that, the rate limits of the network and the ledger, not my workers.
"What would you measure?"p99 of the command path, queue age per dependency, oldest unprocessed deadline, outbox lag, index lag.
"How does the case store scale?"By case id, because no transaction spans two cases. Key on case id from day one. Split for failover size, not for writes.
"One case with thousands of events?"The one hot shape. Cap events per case with policy; the snapshot means reads never replay the history.
"The deadline sweeper is slow."Index on (status, due_at), bounded batches, claims with SKIP LOCKED. The timer takes the normal path, so the sweeper sees only stragglers.
"The network or the ledger throttles you."Concurrency caps and backpressure per dependency. Queue age per dependency on the dashboard.
"80 TB of evidence a month?"Object storage doesn't run out. The limits are scan throughput and egress: scan on its own queue, short-lived URLs, never proxy bytes through the case service.
"Bigger batch files?"Numbered parts under one run. Stream the build; parse inbound files by row range in parallel; the claim stays one transaction.
"Add a queue in front for spikes?"No. At 8 writes / s it's a component with no job, and one more place to lose a command.
"Can you cache anything?"The policy, because it's versioned: same inputs and version, same answer. Precompute eligibility at case open. Never the case.

"How would you improve performance?"

This question is about latency on the customer path, not about scale. Only three things on the command path must be synchronous: permission, the key and version check, and the one commit. The ledger, the network, search and notifications already run after the commit. So the customer waits for one indexed read and one commit. If p99 is bad, look in this order:

  1. A hot partition.
  2. A lock held across a slow call.
  3. A synchronous call that shouldn't be on the path.

Say it

"Performance here means the command path, and that path is one indexed read plus one commit. Policy is precomputed at case open, and the ledger and network calls are already after the commit. If p99 is bad, I look for a hot partition, then a lock held across a slow call, then a synchronous call that shouldn't be there. I wouldn't cache the case; a cached case is how two operators decide it twice."

10. If the network is a file over FTP

A 2026 candidate got this version. Write the disputes to a CSV, upload it by FTP to Mastercard four times a day, and read the answers back from a file.

The sentence that frames it

"The file is just a slower, batched version of an external call. Durable intent before sending, a stable identity per row and per file, answers recorded as events, and everything unanswered aged in a reconciliation queue."

What stays and what changes

Everything up to the outbox stays. The six nouns, the one commit and the keys are the same. The file is a delivery mechanism, not a new kind of truth. Only the meaning of "send" changes. It is one row in a file that leaves at 14:00, not one HTTP call.

The customer sees one difference. An answer takes hours, not seconds. Between the upload and the answer, the row is "awaiting answer". It is not failed and not done. Ana sees "sent to the card network, awaiting response".

On the board

Draw stages 0 and 1 as usual. In stage 2, draw this lane in place of the Card network box. Follow the square numbers.

Step by step: the file lane

Write on it: "claim rows: one transaction" and the run id "2026-09-06-14" in the batch run. "file name = run id" in build and upload. The row key "NB-CB-812-1" in the network box.

Say it while you draw

"Bad rows go to quarantine with the raw line, and the rest of the file still goes on. Every sent row waits in a reconciliation queue until its answer closes it. The age of the oldest item is what pages someone."

Then go back to section 5 and draw stage 4, the deadlines, and stage 5, the read side and the evidence.

Four new nouns

NounWhat it is
BatchRunOne scheduled cycle. Id from the date and the slot, like 2026-09-06-14. File name, row count, checksum, status: BUILDING, UPLOADED, ACKNOWLEDGED, FAILED. Written before the file leaves.
BatchRowOne case's line in one file. Its row id is the NetworkSubmission reference, like NB-CB-812-1. Status: QUEUED, SENT, ANSWERED, REJECTED.
InboundFileA file we received: name, checksum, arrival time, rows parsed, rows quarantined. The original bytes stay in object storage, untouched.
ReconciliationItemA row we sent that has no answer yet, with the time we sent it. Aged all the time.

One day, walked through

  1. 13:58, queued. CB-812 is eligible. Its Submit command created NB-CB-812-1 in state QUEUED. Two hundred other cases wait with it.
  2. 14:00, claim. The batch job creates run 2026-09-06-14. One transaction claims every QUEUED row with a conditional update. A second job that starts by accident claims zero rows.
  3. 14:00, build. The job writes the CSV from the claimed rows: sorted by row id, fixed columns, fixed encoding. It stores the file and its checksum. The same rows always give the same bytes.
  4. 14:01, upload. Send the file by FTP under a name made from the run id. Mark the run UPLOADED. Each row becomes a reconciliation item.
  5. 14:01, tell the case. Write one event per row, SUBMITTED_IN_BATCH 2026-09-06-14, through the normal command path. Close the "submit by" deadline. Open "expect an answer by".
  6. Next day, 07:00, the answer. Download the response file, checksum it and store the original. If its file id is already known, stop: it is the same file twice.
  7. 07:02, apply. Each good row becomes a case command with the key file-id:row-id. CB-812 gets NETWORK_ACKED, and its deadline moves to the merchant's window. A rejected row sends its case back to an Ops queue. A bad row goes to quarantine.
  8. Every hour, reconcile. A sweeper ages every open item. It alerts on items older than two cycles. It escalates any case near its dispute deadline.

Idempotency at three levels

LevelStable identityWhat it prevents
RowThe NetworkSubmission reference, NB-CB-812-1The same dispute in two files. A row joins only one run while QUEUED.
FileThe run id gives the file name and checksum. The network gives the inbound file id.A retry that creates a second file. A response file processed twice.
RunThe run id from date and slot, with a unique constraintTwo schedulers that both build the 14:00 batch. The second insert fails.

A crash halfway through a response file is safe. On the re-run, rows already applied return their earlier result, because each command carries file-id:row-id. Rows not yet applied are applied once.

The failures they will insert

FailureAnswer in one line
Upload times outUnknown, not failed. List the remote directory for the run's file name. If it's there with the right size and checksum, mark UPLOADED. If not, re-upload the same file under the same name. Never rebuild with a new run id.
Job crashes mid-buildRows are claimed, nothing uploaded. On restart, find the run in BUILDING and rebuild the same bytes. Alert if a run stays in BUILDING too long.
Response file arrives twiceSame file id and checksum: stop. Same name, different checksum: quarantine both, alert, a human decides.

A row for a case we don't knowQuarantine it as UNKNOWN_REFERENCE. Ops resolves it. The platform never invents a case.
The 14:00 slot is missedDeadlines are rows, so nothing is lost. The next run takes everything QUEUED. The sweeper escalates any case whose "submit by" falls before the next slot.
No response file for two daysNothing about our cases is wrong. The oldest reconciliation item crosses its threshold and pages someone.

Say it in 30 to 60 seconds

"Four times a day I claim every case that is ready, in one transaction, into a batch run with an id made from the date and the slot. I build a CSV deterministically from the claimed rows, each row carrying the case's stable submission reference, upload it under a name derived from the run id, and mark the run uploaded. Every row is now 'awaiting answer' and sits in a reconciliation queue. When a response file arrives, I dedupe it by file id, parse it row by row, quarantine bad rows without dropping the file, and turn each good row into a case command keyed by file id plus row id. Anything sent and unanswered ages; the age of the oldest item is what pages someone. A timeout on upload is unknown, not failed: I check the remote directory before I re-upload the same file, and I never rebuild with a new run id."

11. Idempotency: the question, and the answer

Interviewers ask about idempotency in almost every report. Waiting to be asked is a negative signal, so name the key on every arrow before they ask.

How they ask it

  • "What happens if Ana taps 'dispute' twice?"
  • "The app times out and retries. Do you open two cases?"
  • "Your worker calls the ledger, and the call times out. What now?"
  • "How do you make sure a customer is never credited twice?"
  • "Walk me through how this system is idempotent, end to end."

The answer, in about a minute

"Idempotency here isn't one key, it's a stable identity on every boundary. The app makes a request key once per tap and resends it on every retry. The key record goes into the same commit as the case, so a retry gets the same case back. Two separate taps have two keys, so a business uniqueness rule returns the existing case: same transaction, same customer, same reason family, while a case is live. Every money action has its own key, like CB-812:PROVISIONAL_CREDIT:v1. So if the ledger times out, I retry with the same key, and it can't credit twice. The network gets the same submission reference on every retry. A response file is deduped by file id, and each row is applied under file id plus row id. And every consumer of my events dedupes by event id."

Six boundaries, one stable identity each

BoundaryStable identity, and where it is checkedWhat it prevents
App → case service, a retryThe request key, made once per tap. Checked by: the idempotency record, unique on (caller, key), in the same commit as the case.A retry that opens a second case
Two separate tapsBusiness uniqueness: transaction, customer, reason family, live case. Checked by: a uniqueness rule in the case database.Two live cases for one purchase. Not the transaction id alone: partial disputes are legitimate.
Case → ledgerThe action key, CB-812:PROVISIONAL_CREDIT:v1. Checked by: the ledger, unique on the key.A second credit. A reversal has its own key, so it is not a retry.
Case → networkThe submission reference, NB-CB-812-1. Checked by: the network. Query by the reference before any resubmit.A second dispute at the network
Network file → caseThe file id, then file-id:row-id. Checked by: InboundFile, and the key of each row's command.A response file applied twice
Our events → consumersThe event id. Checked by: each consumer.A notification or a search update applied twice

How the key check works

  1. The app makes the request key once, when Ana taps, and saves it. On a retry, it sends the same key.
  2. The case service looks up (caller, key) in the idempotency table.
  3. If the key is new, the service runs the command. The same commit writes the key record, a hash of the request and the result.
  4. If the key exists with the same request hash, the service returns the stored result. It changes nothing.
  5. If the key exists with a different request hash, the service returns a conflict. The caller used one key for two intentions.
  6. If two retries arrive at the same moment, the unique constraint lets one insert win. The other one reads the stored result.

The follow-up questions

They askAnswer in one line
"Why not keep the keys in Redis?"Then the key and the case live in two systems. A crash between the two writes loses the key, or saves a key with no case. One Postgres commit holds both.
"Why isn't the ledger key the case id?"One case needs several money actions. With the case id as the key, the reversal looks like a retry of the credit, and the ledger refuses it.
"The ledger times out. Do you retry?"Yes, with the same key. A timeout is unknown, not failed. The case stays in "credit pending" until it sees a result. Never a new key.
"The network times out."Unknown. Query by the same reference before any resubmit. If the network has no safe resubmit, escalate to a human.
"How long do you keep the keys?"Longer than any retry: a window like 24–72 hours for customer commands. The business uniqueness rule covers the rest of the case's life.
"Kafka has exactly-once. Isn't that enough?"It covers Kafka to Kafka and stops at your database write. Consumers still dedupe by event id.

What I would not add, and why

  • A separate store for idempotency keys. The key must be in the same commit as the case.
  • A queue in front of the case database. At 8 writes a second it has no job. It is one more place to lose a command.
  • A workflow engine as the source of truth. If the company has one, use it for timers and retries. The case service still owns transitions, policy, audit and idempotency.
  • Sharding on day one. One primary handles ten times this load. Split by case id later, for failover size, not for writes.
  • NoSQL "for scale". The numbers don't ask for it.

Say it

"I'll use Postgres for the case store, because one commit has to hold the event, the snapshot, the deadlines, the key and the outbox row, and at eight writes a second one primary is plenty. Kafka carries events after the commit, S3 holds the evidence, OpenSearch serves Ops search, and everything else is stateless workers on Kubernetes."

If they ask about Nubank's own stack

Nubank's postings list Clojure, Kafka, Datomic, DynamoDB, AWS, Kubernetes and Prometheus. You don't need to design in Clojure. Say one sentence in their language, once, when it fits.

In their words

"Your Datomic transaction function is where my check-and-write becomes one atomic operation. Kafka carries the outbox events between services, and every consumer dedupes on a correlation id, which is how you already do it."

"Why Postgres and not DynamoDB?"

"Either can work. Postgres gives me one transaction across five tables, unique constraints, and the deadline index with no extra design. With DynamoDB I'd use transactional writes with conditions, and I'd design the due-deadline query as a secondary index up front."

13. Rollout, in three lines

One country, one network, one reason family. First shadow: run the new rules on real inputs with every side effect blocked, compare. Then enable network submissions behind a kill switch, then money behind its own. Legal owns the rules, Ops owns the queues, the ledger team owns money, we own the mechanics.

14. Lead signals, in their words

The Buenos Aires Lead posting names four things: trade-offs said out loud, failure-domain isolation, observability, and disaster recovery. Here is where each one lives in this design, and the sentence to say.

Communicating trade-offs

A trade-off has three parts: the choice, what it buys, and what it costs. Say all three, every time. Three choices carry this design.

  • The outbox instead of a synchronous call. It buys a case that is never half done. It costs a worker fleet and in-flight states, such as "credit pending", that the product has to show.
  • Events plus a snapshot instead of a status column. It buys the audit trail and "the case as of any date". It costs two writes per command.
  • Case state apart from money state. It buys honesty: "won" is a decision, "paid" is a fact. It costs a reconciliation of three truths before the case closes.

Say it

"I chose the outbox over calling the ledger inside the request. I gain a case that can't be half done. I lose immediacy: Ana sees 'credit pending' for a few seconds, and the product has to show that state."

Failure-domain isolation

A failure domain is the set of things that break together. A Lead keeps each domain small, so that a failure stays local and the rest of the platform keeps working.

  • One domain per dependency. The network, the ledger, storage, search and notifications each sit behind their own adapter and outbox rows. When one of them dies, only its work waits.
  • One kill switch per side effect. Network submissions and money actions stop separately. Committed work stays visible while a switch is off.
  • The case store splits by case id when failover size or isolation demands it, not for throughput. No transaction spans two cases, so a split never breaks a rule.

Say it

"Each dependency is its own failure domain. If the network dies, only submissions queue. Cases still open, operators still decide, and credits still reach the ledger with their keys."

Observability

Measure the things that hurt a customer or break a legal deadline. Raw error counts come second.

  • Five numbers to watch:
    • Queue age per dependency.
    • The oldest unprocessed deadline, and its time to breach.
    • The age of the oldest outbox row.
    • The p99 of the command path.
    • Reconciliation exceptions.
  • One id across everything. Each command carries a correlation id into every event, message and external call. So one id tells the whole story of one case.
  • The event history is the first debugging tool. "What happened to CB-812?" is a query, not an investigation.

Say it

"When one case is wrong, I read its events. When many are wrong, I read the dependency dashboards. External latency up with healthy queues means a dependency. Queue age up with normal latency means us."

Disaster recovery

Disaster recovery has two numbers. The recovery point is how much committed work you may lose. The recovery time is how long until the platform works again. State both for each disaster, and say who decides.

  • A zone dies. The synchronous replica in another zone takes over in seconds. Recovery point: zero. During the switch, commands get "try again" and reads keep working.
  • A region dies. An asynchronous replica in a second region takes over, and a human decides when. Recovery point: the last few seconds of commands. The apps retry unanswered commands with their keys. Reconciliation with the network and the ledger finds any side effect that went out but was not recorded.
  • Bad code or a bad policy corrupts cases. History is immutable, so recovery is fix-forward. Freeze the version, find the cases by version, and write correcting events. Rebuild the snapshots from the events.
  • The deadline rows survive any restore. A restored database still knows every legal obligation, because deadlines are rows, not timers in memory.

The region numbers are an assumption to say out loud. Synchronous replication across regions would make the recovery point zero, but every commit would pay a 50 to 150 ms hop. For a workflow that runs over weeks, a few seconds of exposure that reconciliation repairs is the better trade.

Say it

"Losing a zone costs us seconds and no data. Losing a region is a human decision: the replica there can miss the last few seconds of commands. The apps retry with the same keys, and reconciliation against the network and the ledger catches anything that went out but wasn't recorded."

15. Never say

  • "Opening the case credits the customer." (Credit is a separate policy decision.)
  • "The scheduler will handle the deadline." (The row is the obligation.)
  • "Resubmit with a fresh reference." (That can open a second dispute.)
  • "The case is won, so she's been paid." (Decision and settlement are different.)
  • "Roll back the policy and fix the statuses." (Fix forward, with events.)
  • "It'll be a lot of data, so we need something scalable." (Say the number and what it decides.)
  • "Let's cache the case." (A cached decision input is a stale one.)
  • "Retry the upload with a fresh file." (That resends every dispute in it.)
  • "Reject the whole file if a row is bad." (That blocks 199 good cases for one.)
  • "The cron job handles the schedule." (The claim is a transaction. The cron is only a trigger.)
  • "We'd add an idempotency key." (Say whose key, where it lives and what it prevents.)
  • "Retry with a new key after a timeout." (That is how a customer is credited twice.)
