The Hello Interview method, applied to Nubank's hour

Nubank's interviewers pointed you at one video: Evan King's ad-click aggregator walkthrough. This guide takes the road map he follows, step by step. It shows every move he makes in the 56 minutes and why the interviewer in him rewards it. Then it runs the same steps over your chargeback and ledger designs, so you walk in with one method, not two.

Built 1 October 2026 from the video transcript (youtu.be/1oFVUT4_Yy0), its written answer key (hellointerview.com/youtube/ad-click-aggregator) and Hello Interview's delivery framework page. Companion to Nubank's prep document, decoded, which names the video in section 11.

1. The end result: the road map on one page

Read this first. Everything else in the guide is one of these steps, worked out.

Four steps, each feeding the next. Step 1, requirements: features, then qualities with numbers. Step 2, a thin bridge: entities and API, or interface and data flow. Step 3, a high-level design that satisfies only the features. Step 4, deep dives that take the qualities one at a time.

The interviewer who recommended this to you grades the order as much as the boxes. Your seven stages already fit inside it. The video adds two habits: say which step you are on, and draw nothing until step three.

Two flavours of the same road map

Evan King opens by saying the road map has two variants. A product design question asks for something a person uses: Ticketmaster, Uber, Dropbox. There, the bridge between requirements and the drawing is core entities and API.

An infrastructure design question asks for a pipeline rather than a screen: an ad-click aggregator, a web crawler, a reconciliation engine. Entities and endpoints make less sense there. The bridge becomes system interface (what comes in, what goes out) and data flow (the ordered steps that turn one into the other). Both variants start with requirements and end with a high-level design plus deep dives.

Say which flavour your prompt is in the first two minutes. Chargebacks and the ledger are product-shaped: a customer or an operator acts on a case or a transfer. So you list nouns and endpoints. An ETL for fraud features, a reconciliation job, a file exchange with the card network, or any "count things per minute" prompt is infrastructure-shaped. So you list inputs, outputs and the steps between them.

What each step feeds

The sentence he repeats most: each section fuels the next. Functional requirements become the inputs, outputs and data flow. The data flow becomes the high-level design, drawn in the same order. The non-functional requirements become the deep dives, taken one at a time. When you get lost mid-interview, this is the thread to pick up: "which requirement am I serving right now?"

2. Requirements: features, then qualities with numbers

Minutes 3 to 12. The step the video spends the most care on, and the step Nubank's PDF grades hardest.

Functional requirements are features, written as expectations

He writes them as what a user expects of the system. The user may be a person, an operator, another team, or a machine. Two lines for the ad-click aggregator:

  1. Users click on an ad and are redirected to the advertiser's website.
  2. Advertisers query click metrics over time, with a minimum granularity of one minute.

Then a short list he labels below the line (out of scope): ad targeting and serving, cross-device tracking, integration with offline channels. He says two things about that list that matter for you. First, write it only if it comes naturally. Candidates who strain to produce out-of-scope items sound like padding. Second, the point of the list is agreement. You are getting the interviewer to nod at a boundary, so say it aloud and wait for the nod.

Ask about scale before you write the qualities

Nubank's section-11 note already flagged this move, and the transcript shows why it matters. He stops and asks the interviewer "how many ads at any one time, how many clicks per second at peak". He gets 10 million ads and 10,000 clicks per second at peak. Only then does he write the non-functional list.

The written version adds the arithmetic. Peak is roughly ten times average, so about 1,000 clicks a second on average. Times 86,400 seconds, that is about 100 million clicks a day. Every later decision quotes one of those numbers.

Your arithmetic table in section 4 of the decoded guide is the same habit. The order is what the video adds: numbers come before the qualities, because the qualities are written in terms of them.

Non-functional requirements: in context, and quantified

The trap he names explicitly

Many candidates write "scalability, low latency, fault tolerance, security" as bare words. He calls that "wholly uninteresting". Instead: pick the qualities that are uniquely relevant to this system, put each in the context of this system, and quantify it where you can.

His list for the ad-click aggregator, with the context and the number attached:

  • Scalable to a peak of 10,000 clicks per second.
  • Low latency on the advertisers' analytics queries: under one second.
  • Fault tolerant with high data integrity: no click is lost, because clicks are what advertisers are billed for.
  • As real time as possible: a click should be visible in the metrics as soon as it can be, within the one-minute granularity.
  • Idempotent click tracking: one user clicking one ad placement ten times, or a bot doing it, counts once.

Below the line: spam detection beyond idempotency, demographic slicing, conversion tracking. He admits on camera that "fault tolerant" could have been put in context better. Copy that candour. It shows he checks the list against the system instead of reciting it.

3. Interface and data flow, or entities and API

Minutes 12 to 15. A thin step. It exists so that the drawing has something to follow.

For a pipeline prompt: inputs, outputs, then the ordered steps

The system interface is one line each side. Inputs: click data from users, and queries from advertisers. Outputs: a redirect for the user, and aggregated click metrics for the advertiser. He says on camera that for this question it was almost too simple to write, and that you should do it only if it helps you. Keep that honesty. But note that he still did it, because it makes the next list easy.

The data flow is a numbered list of the steps from input to output. His six:

  1. Click data comes into the system.
  2. The user is redirected.
  3. The click is validated (this is where idempotency will live).
  4. The raw click is logged.
  5. Clicks are aggregated into a read-optimised shape.
  6. Advertisers query the aggregated data.

Then he checks the list against the features: steps 1, 2 and 6 are the two functional requirements. That check is the whole point of the step. If a feature has no step, you forgot something. If a step serves no feature, it is gold-plating.

For a product prompt: nouns, then the operations on them

Core entities are the nouns the features need. The API is the two or three operations that act on them. The framework page gives two rules: plural resource names, and the caller's identity comes from the authentication token, never the request body.

The bridge in one breath, for any prompt

"Here are the nouns, here are the operations on them, and here's the key each operation carries." Thirty seconds. Then pick up the pen.

4. High-level design: satisfy the features, nothing more

Minutes 15 to 27. The video's most quotable rule, its time marks, and the ladder of bad, good and great.

The goal of the high-level design is to satisfy the functional requirements, following the data flow in order. That's it. Qualities come later. He gives the time marks directly. A mid-level candidate should reach this step by about 15 minutes in. A senior or staff candidate should reach it by about 10, "so there's plenty of time for the high-level design and the deep dives". On Nubank's clock the first three minutes are the interviewer's, so that is minute 13 to 15 for you.

The redirect: client side versus 302

He starts with the easy feature, and still finds a trade-off in it. The browser gets ads from an Ad Placement Service, backed by an ads store with ad id, redirect URL and creative. The service is out of scope, but he draws it as a box so the data entering the system has a source.

OptionHowWhy not, or why
Client-side redirectSend the redirect URL with the ad. The browser navigates straight to it and POSTs the click in parallel.Anyone with an ad blocker, or anyone who reads the DOM, goes to the advertiser without the click ever reaching you. Someone would write a browser extension for it.
Server-side 302Send only the ad id. The click hits your click processor, which logs it, looks up the redirect URL and answers with a 302.Every click passes through you, and you can append tracking parameters. The cost is one more hop on the user's path, so the processor must be fast.

Notice the shape. The "simple" option has a correctness hole, the "robust" option has a latency cost, and he names both. That is the trade-off signal in Nubank's rubric, shown on the smallest possible decision.

Store and query: bad, good, great

Then the second feature, and here he does something you should copy deliberately. He says a senior candidate would not start from the worst version. He builds it anyway, so the viewer sees why each rung exists. In your interview the same ladder is your v1 → v2 → v3 story. Show the first rung for thirty seconds, break it yourself, climb.

BadClick processor writes each click event (event id, ad id, user id, timestamp) to a click database. A query service runs SELECT COUNT(*) … GROUP BY over the ad id and time range. Why it fails: at 10k inserts a second the aggregating query is "super super slow". Cassandra suffers most, but Postgres and DynamoDB suffer too once the window opens. It breaks the under-one-second quality. He is explicit: "this would not pass as a high-level design at any level, including mid-level". Some things are too bad even for a first draft.
GoodKeep the write-optimised event store. Cassandra: commit log, memtable, SSTables. It is great for a row by key, bad for ranges and aggregations. Add a cron that starts a Spark map-reduce every five minutes. It aggregates by ad id and minute and writes (ad id, minute, clicks) into a read-optimised OLAP store. The query service reads the OLAP store. The arithmetic: 10k clicks/s × 300 s = 3 million events per batch × ~100 bytes = 300 MB. That fits one machine, so Spark is chosen for the framework, not the volume. Why two databases: contention and fault isolation. Writes and reads stop competing for the same threads. If the write path breaks, advertisers still read. If the read path breaks, clicks still log and you backfill. Why it's only good: advertisers always see data minutes old. A spike makes the next batch longer than the batch interval, so lag grows exactly when a big launch needs freshness.
GreatReplace the batch with a stream. The click processor writes to Kafka or Kinesis. A stream processor (Flink, or Spark Streaming) keeps per-ad counters in memory over a one-minute aggregation window. It writes each closed window to the OLAP store. Why Flink and not hand-rolled consumers: event-time windows, so out-of-order clicks land in the right minute, watermarks that know when a window can close, exactly-once semantics, and state recovery. The lever that makes it real time: a flush interval, say ten seconds, writes partial counts for the open minute. A dashboard then shows the current minute as a dotted, incomplete bar that fills in. He says many candidates start their high-level design here, and that's fine. If you start at "good", he will say "I don't like that five-minute delay, do better", and you climb.

The numbers in your pivot guide differ, on purpose

Section 6 of Pivot insurance and section 8 of the decoded guide sketch this prompt at 10k clicks/s average and 100k at peak. That is a stress number chosen to force sharding. The video's numbers are 10k at peak and about 1k average.

If Nubank hands you the prompt, ask for the number and use theirs. If they don't give one, say which you are assuming and why it changes the design. At 10k peak one well-tuned Postgres or DynamoDB takes the writes, and the problem is the query shape. At 100k peak the ingest itself needs partitioning.

The final board

How this is Nubank's "begin simple, then iterate"

Nubank's PDF wants "my first idea is X, the pros are, the cons are, we tackle that next by". The ladder is that sentence three times. The video and the PDF differ on the floor. He will not accept the "bad" rung as a high-level design, even from a mid-level candidate. So your v1 should be his "good": a working design with a named lag, not a design that cannot serve the feature.

Section 6 of the chargeback guide and section 4 of the ledger guide now do exactly this. The one-table rung is named in a sentence and not drawn. The design on the board is the atomic-commit version, and its gaps are listed as the deep-dive menu.

5. Deep dives: one quality at a time

Minutes 27 to 53. He literally scrolls back up to the non-functional list and takes them in order. Do the same, out loud.

Each dive below has three parts: what he does, what the interviewer in him is looking for, and where the same move already lives in your chargeback or ledger design. That third part lets you say "this is the same shape as…" and collect the architecture-fundamentals signal.

As real time as possible

What he does. Names the problem with the "good" rung: a five-minute batch means a five-minute-old dashboard. Tries the obvious fix, run Spark more often, and finds its floor: job start-up overhead means you cannot run it every few seconds. Only then introduces the stream and Flink.

Two terms get written on the board. The aggregation window is the period over which events are grouped: one minute. The flush interval is how often partial results are written out: ten seconds. The written version adds two points. Decreasing Flink's window is realistic where increasing Spark's frequency is not. And a window plus a flush gives you both: minute-boundary numbers that are final, and a current minute that fills in.

What he is looking for. That you exhausted the cheap fix before reaching for the new component. "Run it more often" and its limit is the trade-off. The stream is the consequence.

Your version. The chargeback timer plus sweeper is the same two-lever idea. The timer gives freshness, the sweeper gives completeness, and you say what each one's interval costs. In the ledger, projections are the flush. The account aggregate is updated at commit. The search projection lags by seconds and says so.

10k clicks a second

What he does. Walks the path of one click and scales each box. The click processor and the placement service scale horizontally on CPU or memory thresholds, behind a load balancer or managed API gateway. He says the multiple-boxes drawing is optional. Say it aloud instead.

The stream needs sharding, because a Kinesis shard takes 1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s, whichever comes first. Shard by ad id, the natural key, so all of one ad's clicks land on one shard. Flink scales as one task per shard. No two tasks share an ad id, so there is no distributed contention.

The OLAP store shards by advertiser id, not ad id, because the common query is "all my ads". He says this is the moment to "do a little bit of math" on the aggregated store's size, then skips it on camera. You should not skip it.

Then the hot shard. Nike launches an ad with LeBron James. Every click goes to one shard, latency climbs, and in the worst case data drops.

The fix is the celebrity pattern. For popular ads, known by spend or recent volume, the click processor appends a random suffix, so the partition key becomes ad id:0–N. That breaks the one-task-per-ad property, so a single Flink task reads the set of shards for that ad. Flink supports that. The written version adds that Flink strips the suffix before writing and upserts with a sum, so writes from different partitions combine correctly.

What he is looking for. The order: horizontal first, then the component with a hard limit, then the key, then the key's failure mode. He calls the first part "straightforward, obvious stuff you do in most interviews" and the hot shard "where this gets interesting". Spend your time accordingly.

Your version. The five-step scaling answer in section 11 of the ledger guide is this walk: what grew, the measurable bottleneck, scale what scales, say what doesn't, protect the invariants. The hot shard is your hot account: a merchant or the clearing account taking every write. The ledger guide's answer, scope the clearing account per shard with a deterministic lock order, is the same move as the suffix.

Never lose a click

What he does. Three layers. First, the stream itself. Kafka replicates across brokers, Kinesis across availability zones. If managed, assume it is up.

Second, a retention policy of seven days. A Flink task that dies comes back and re-reads from its last committed cursor. Nothing was consumed away.

Third, a reconciliation. A connector (Kinesis Firehose, Kafka Connect) dumps every raw event to S3. A daily Spark job re-aggregates the raw log. A reconciliation worker compares its result with the OLAP store, trusts the batch, and overwrites the difference. It also alerts the team, so a rising discrepancy count is itself a signal that something broke.

Between the second and third layers is the moment the transcript is most useful for. Candidates, he says, reliably propose Flink checkpointing because "they've read it in a book somewhere". Checkpointing means periodic state snapshots to S3, every ten or fifteen minutes by default.

He pushes back. With a one-minute window there is at most a minute of state to lose: 10,000 × 60 events to replay from the stream. So a checkpoint halfway through the minute buys nothing and costs S3 writes. His rule of thumb: windows under five minutes, don't checkpoint. And he says why it matters for the grade: "these identifications that go against the grain are really effective ways to show seniority."

He closes with Lambda and Kappa. Kappa: everything is a stream, one real-time layer, the design before reconciliation. Lambda: a batch layer that is correct and a speed layer that is fast but unguaranteed, with the batch overwriting.

His final design is neither and both. When candidates ask "is that allowed?", his answer is that textbook patterns are guidelines and hybrids are "incredibly practical". In his words, "practicality should almost always be favoured over abstract purity". Said once, that sentence is worth a level.

What he is looking for. One thing you could have skipped and said why. Checkpointing is the planted one. Find yours.

Your version. This is the three-truths reconciliation of the chargeback close and the outbox-plus-replay of the ledger. The raw log plays the event store. Your planted pushback is already in the mock: "I'd push back on caching the case". Add the checkpointing one for a pipeline prompt. For the ledger: "I wouldn't checkpoint the projection builder either; it rebuilds from the log, that's what the log is for."

One click per impression

The video runs out of time before this dive and says so. The written version carries it. Nubank's security-and-abuse signal makes it the one you should not skip.

What the answer key does. First the tempting fix: require login and dedupe on user id plus ad id. It fails twice. Not everyone is logged in. And it dedupes the wrong thing: retargeting shows the same ad to the same user on purpose, and each showing should be clickable once.

The unit of idempotency is the impression, one rendering of one ad to one user. So the ad placement service mints an impression id per rendering. It signs the id together with the ad id with an HMAC under a server secret: microseconds, no asymmetric crypto. It sends the signed id with the ad. The click carries it back, and the click processor verifies the signature. That stops both invented ids and replaying a valid id against a different ad.

Then where to dedupe. Not in Flink, because a duplicate on either side of a minute boundary would count twice. Before the stream, in a cache keyed by impression id.

The order of the two writes is the senior detail: write to the stream first, then mark the cache. A cache failure then costs you a duplicate that reconciliation catches. The other order can lose a click, and lost clicks are unrecoverable. Size: 100 million clicks a day × 16 bytes ≈ 1.6 GB, "tiny". A Redis cluster with a replica and persistence makes it a non-bottleneck.

Your version. This is your idempotency key, with one addition you may not have said aloud. The key is minted by the system and signed, not trusted from the client. In the chargeback platform the semantic action key (case, action, version) is minted server-side. In the ledger the client generates the transfer key, but the server pins the payload to it and rejects a conflicting replay. "Stream first, cache second" is the same ordering as "commit first, then publish from the outbox". The durable write goes first, the convenience write second, and the reconciliation absorbs the gap.

Queries under a second

What the answer key does. Mostly already solved by pre-aggregation. Where it still hurts is a query over weeks or years, which sums millions of minute rows. The fix is a nightly cron that pre-aggregates into daily and weekly tables. The dashboard reads the coarse table and drills down only when asked. He frames it as a form of caching: storage traded for latency on the common query.

Your version. The ledger's account aggregate is exactly this. It is the sum you would otherwise compute over every entry, kept as a row. The log is the source of truth it can be rebuilt from. Same for the chargeback case snapshot beside its events.

The pattern across all five dives

Each one starts from a quality in the list and names what the current board does wrong against it. It tries the cheap fix and states its ceiling. Then it adds exactly one thing. The thing has a cost, and the cost is said. Five times, in order.

Nubank's PDF calls that rhythm "zoom in and out" and "trade-offs, not optimal answers". The mock in the chargeback folder is written in it.

8. Interview sentences, collected

  1. "Requirements first, features then qualities with rough numbers; then the nouns and the operations; then a simple design that serves the features; then the qualities one by one."Minute two. The road map, said once.
  2. "Below the line for today: fraud scoring, the merchant side, the network's internals. Does that boundary work for you?"Features. Get the nod.
  3. "Before I write the qualities, what scale should I design for?"The question that comes before the non-functional list.
  4. "Not just 'low latency': the operator's view of a case under 300 ms, and acceptance of a command under a second. Not just 'scalable': 70,000 cases a day today, so ops throughput before write throughput."Qualities in context, quantified.
  5. "Each of those steps serves one of the features; steps three and four serve none directly, they exist for the qualities, and I'll come back to them in the dives."Data flow checked against the features.
  6. "This first version serves the features. It has three flaws I can see, so let me fix them in order."The high-level design climbing the ladder.
  7. "I could run the batch more often, but there's a floor: the job's start-up cost. So the fix is a different shape, not a shorter interval."Cheap fix exhausted before a new component.
  8. "A lot of candidates would add checkpointing here. With a one-minute window there's at most a minute to replay from the stream, so I'd leave it out and say why."The planted pushback, pipeline flavour.
  9. "Write to the durable thing first, then the convenience thing. A cache failure costs me a duplicate that reconciliation catches; the other order loses a click I can't get back."Ordering of the two writes.
  10. "This is neither Lambda nor Kappa; it's the hybrid that's practical here. The patterns are guidelines; I'd rather be practical than pure."Nuance that reads as seniority.
  11. "Of these five qualities I'll go deepest on two, and say why I chose them."Choosing the dives.
  12. "Let me take that, then return to the step I was on, which is the second dive."Recovery from an interruption, with the step named.
