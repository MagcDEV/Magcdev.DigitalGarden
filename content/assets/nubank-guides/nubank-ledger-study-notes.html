The ledger, end to end

How every piece of Nubank's authoritative-ledger design fits together, written to be understood first and then practised backwards through the seven interview stages.

Compiled from a study session on 4 September 2026, working from the Nubank Lead System Design Study Guide (Mock A: authoritative ledger and transfers). Each section is one question that was asked; the diagram nodes in section 1 correspond to sections 2 through 8. Numbers, the single point of failure, the degradation matrix, schema evolution and caching were added 29 September 2026 from Nubank's own candidate PDF (see Nubank's prep document, decoded).

1. The end result

Explain in simple terms the design of the ledger: how all the pieces work together, so I can work from the beginning and practise mock interviews.

The ledger is a notebook where every money movement is written as a pair of balancing lines, saved in one single database commit, and only after that commit does the rest of the world get told about it.

Everything else in the design follows from that sentence. Here is the complete system you would arrive at by the end of the interview:

The six nouns

Think of a paper ledger book:

AccountA bucket of value in one currency, with a version number: a counter that goes up on every change.
TransactionThe business event: "Ana sent Bruno R$10", stamped with the client's idempotency key. Nubank calls it a movement; either word is fine.
EntryOne signed line against one account. The R$10 transfer is two entries: Ana −10, Bruno +10. They always sum to zero. Entries are never edited; a mistake is fixed with a new, opposite entry that points at the wrong one.
Account aggregateThe cheat-sheet summary so you don't re-add years of entries every time Ana pays: posted balance, held amount, available balance, version. It must always be rebuildable from the entries.
HoldA reservation (card authorization) that lowers available without touching posted.
Outbox rowA note saying "publish this event later."

One transfer, start to finish

  1. Ana's app sends the command with key K1. The network can deliver it twice; the key is what makes "twice" harmless.
  2. The ledger looks up K1. Already seen with the same payload: return the original result and stop. Same key, different payload: deterministic error, never processed.
  3. It checks that both accounts exist, the currency matches, Ana has R$10 available (not just posted), and her account version is the one it read. Two concurrent debits can't both win; one sees a stale version and retries.
  4. In one database transaction it writes the transaction, both entries, the updated aggregates for Ana and Bruno, the idempotency record, and the outbox row. All or nothing. This is the whole trick: save the key and the money in separate steps, and a crash between them lets money move twice.
  5. Commit succeeds, so Ana gets "done". Not because Kafka got a message. Because the database committed.
  6. Afterwards, a worker reads the outbox and publishes to Kafka. Consumers build the transaction feed, send the push notification, feed fraud models. If Kafka is down for six hours, money is still right; the outbox catches up later. Consumers deduplicate because they may see a message twice.

Why it's shaped this way: the three failure moments

  • Timeout before commit: nothing moved, a retry with K1 is just a first attempt.
  • Timeout after commit: the retry with K1 returns the same answer. You never infer the outcome from a timeout, and you never send a fresh key.
  • After commit but event lost: the outbox republishes.

And the one rule that protects everything: if the ledger can't verify available funds (shard down, failover in progress), it refuses the debit rather than approving from a cache. A few seconds of "try again" beats a loss you can't recover.

The one extension: when payer and payee live in different databases

You can't update two databases in one commit, so you add a clearing account as a waiting room.

Each leg has its own stable key, and a durable progress record says which leg is done. Between legs the customer sees Processing and the money is fully accounted for: it's in clearing. If leg B can never complete, policy decides whether to reverse leg A with a new linked entry. Monitor clearing by amount and by age of the oldest item.

How this maps back to the 7 stages for your mocks

The end picture above is stage 4 plus the stage 5 extension. Working backwards:

The one sentence that ties it together

"Money becomes durable in the atomic store; events describe that fact afterwards."

If you can say that, and defend why the idempotency key lives inside the same commit, most of the follow-ups fall out naturally.

2. The transfer command and the idempotency key

What exactly is in a transfer command, and why does the client generate the idempotency key?

What's in the command

{
  "idempotency_key": "K1",
  "source_account": "acc_ana",
  "destination_account": "acc_bruno",
  "amount": 1000,
  "currency": "BRL",
  "type": "INTERNAL_TRANSFER",
  "reference": "Rent split",
  "requested_at": "2026-09-04T14:02:11Z"
}

Plus, from the transport layer rather than the body: the authenticated caller (which app, which customer, which service), and optionally the account version the client expects, if you want clients to take part in concurrency control.

  • Amount is an integer in minor units (1000 = R$10.00), never a float. Floats round; money doesn't.
  • Currency is explicit even for a single-currency product. The ledger balances within a currency, and the day you add a second one you don't want to migrate every command.
  • Type decides which posting rule applies: an internal transfer is two entries; a PIX-out is three (customer, clearing, and later the rail's settlement account); a purchase may carry a fee leg.
  • No balances, no entries, no transaction id. The client says what it wants; the ledger decides what gets written. The transaction id is minted by the ledger on commit and returned in the response.

Why the client generates the key

Because the client is the only party that knows whether it is retrying.

Play out the alternative, where the ledger mints the key on arrival. Ana taps "send". The request reaches the ledger, it commits, and the response is lost in the network. Her app sees a timeout and retries. The ledger sees what looks like a brand-new request, mints a new key, and moves R$10 again. The server had no way to know the two arrivals were one intent.

Now the client generates it. Ana taps "send"; the app generates K1 once, stores it locally, and sends it on every retry of that tap. The first arrival commits and stores K1 with the result. The second arrival finds K1 and returns the original transaction. If the app crashes mid-retry, it still has K1 on disk and can ask "what happened to K1?", and the ledger answers with certainty.

The general principle: the identity of a business intention is born where the intention is born. For the mobile app, that's the tap. For an internal service calling the ledger, it's derived from that service's durable state: a chargeback platform uses case_id:PROVISIONAL_CREDIT:v1, a transfer saga uses transfer_id:LEG_B. Never uuid() on each retry.

What the ledger does with it

The key is scoped: unique on (caller, idempotency_key), so two different apps can both use "K1" without colliding. Stored alongside it: a hash of the payload and the resulting transaction id. On arrival:

  • Key not found → process normally, store the key in the same commit.
  • Key found, payload hash matches → return the stored transaction, don't touch money.
  • Key found, payload hash differs → return a deterministic conflict error. The caller reused one identity for two different intentions. That's a client bug, and the safe move is to process neither: don't apply the new payload, don't overwrite the old result.

Keys need a retention window so the table doesn't grow forever: say 24–72 hours for customer commands, longer for service commands. The window must outlast any plausible retry.

What the interviewer is testing

Whether you separate transport delivery from business effect. Kafka, HTTP and the mobile network can all deliver a message twice; that's normal and unfixable. Effectively-once is not something the network gives you. The ledger makes it true by keying every command on an identity chosen by whoever formed the intent.

Interview sentence

"The client owns the key because only the client knows a retry is a retry. The ledger stores the key atomically with the posting, so the same key always returns the same transaction and a conflicting payload is rejected, never reinterpreted."

3. Validation and the account version

What does the ledger validate before committing, and why is the account version needed? (Asked four times; the "Validate" box in the diagram fires it on every tap.)

What gets validated

  1. Idempotency key. Seen before with the same payload → return the old result; same key, different payload → conflict.
  2. Accounts. Both exist, are open, and are allowed to post this kind of entry (a customer account can't debit the fee-income account, for example).
  3. Currency. Both legs and the transaction are in the same currency and ledger book. Cross-currency is two linked balanced transactions plus rate/rounding accounts, never one "R$10 = $2" sum.
  4. Available funds. Ana's available balance (posted minus open holds) covers R$10. Not posted balance, because a card hold may already be reserving part of it.

All of this happens inside the same database transaction as the write, reading the current committed state, not a cache.

Why the account version

Picture two requests hitting Ana's account at the same instant: a R$80 transfer to Bruno and a R$80 card purchase. Ana has R$100. Both read "available = 100", both pass validation, both write. Ana is now at −60. The invariant "no debit from stale funds" just broke, even though each request did everything right on its own.

The version is a counter on the account aggregate that increments on every change. Every write is conditional:

UPDATE account
   SET balance = 20, version = 419
 WHERE id = 'acc_ana' AND version = 418;

The first request wins and bumps the version to 419. The second request's update matches zero rows, so its whole transaction rolls back. It then re-reads (available is now 20), re-validates, and fails cleanly with insufficient funds.

So the version turns "read then write" into "read, then write only if nothing changed since I read". That is optimistic concurrency: no long-held locks, no deadlocks between shards, and a cheap retry because nothing committed.

Two things interviewers push on

  • Why not just row-lock? You can. SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the account row serialises the same way. The version is friendlier when contention is low; locking is simpler when you already hold a transaction. Section 10 has the full comparison.
  • Why not use timestamps? Clocks on different machines disagree. The version is the authoritative order per account; wall-clock time is metadata. Order is part of correctness, because a balance is a cumulative sum over ordered entries.

What the version is not

Not a global sequence number, and not a timestamp. It is per account, which is exactly why it scales: Ana's version and Bruno's never contend, and a busy merchant account can't slow down a quiet one. When a transaction touches two accounts, check both versions in the same commit and take them in a fixed order (by account id) so opposite-direction transfers can't deadlock.

60-second recap, as you'd say it in the room

Inside the same transaction it checks the idempotency key, that both accounts exist and may take this entry type, that currency and book match, and that available funds (posted minus holds) cover the amount.

Two R$80 debits race against Ana's R$100. Both read available = 100, both pass the check. Without a version, both commit and Ana is at −60. With a version, each write is WHERE version = 418; the first wins and bumps to 419, the second matches zero rows, rolls back, re-reads available = 20, and fails cleanly. The version converts "read then write" into "write only if nothing changed since I read."

Interview sentence

"The hold check, funds check and aggregate update are atomic with the account version, so two concurrent debits can both read the same balance but only one can commit against it."

4. The single atomic commit

What rows are written together, and why must they be in one transaction?

The five rows

For "Ana sends Bruno R$10, key K1", one BEGIN ... COMMIT writes:

  1. Transaction: tx_9182, type INTERNAL_TRANSFER, amount 1000 BRL, status POSTED, created_at.
  2. Two entries: (tx_9182, acc_ana, −1000) and (tx_9182, acc_bruno, +1000). Together they sum to zero.
  3. Two account aggregate updates: Ana: posted 10000 → 9000, available recomputed, version 418 → 419. Bruno: posted +1000, version bumped. Each UPDATE ... WHERE version = <what I read>.
  4. Idempotency record: (caller, "K1", payload_hash, tx_9182). Unique constraint on (caller, key).
  5. Outbox row: event TRANSFER_POSTED, payload, partition key acc_ana, published_at NULL.

The reads that gate the writes (key lookup, account status, currency, available funds) happen inside the same transaction. They see committed state, and the conditional update guards against anyone changing it underneath you.

Why one transaction, row by row

Take each pair out of the commit and watch what a crash in the gap does:

If you split……a crash in the gap produces
Entries without aggregateJournal says Ana has 9000, balance row says 10000. The next debit is authorized against money that's gone.
Aggregate without entriesBalance dropped by 10 with no record of why. Auditors can't explain it, reconciliation can't rebuild it, no reversal target.
Money without keyCommit succeeds, crash before saving K1. Retry with K1 → never seen → processed again → R$20 moved. The most common way ledgers double-post.
Key without moneyK1 saved, crash before posting. Retry finds K1, returns "already done" for a transaction that doesn't exist. Ana sees "Sent"; nothing moved.
Money without outboxTransfer committed, event never exists. Feed, notification and fraud never see it, and there is nothing to retry.
Outbox without moneyWorker publishes TRANSFER_POSTED for a transfer that rolled back. Downstream acts on a ghost.

Each gap is a distinct class of financial bug. One transaction removes all six at once. That is why the design insists on a single strongly consistent write boundary before any talk of sharding.

What "atomic" buys you that nothing else does

Atomicity means the database either applies all five writes or none. Combined with the unique constraint on the key and the conditional update on the version, you get three guarantees from one primitive:

  • Effectively-once: the key and the money are inseparable.
  • Balance integrity: entries and aggregate can never disagree at rest.
  • Guaranteed publication: an event exists if and only if the money moved.

Try to get those from three separate systems (a key-value store for keys, a database for balances, Kafka for events) and you are writing a distributed transaction or a saga to glue them together, for a problem one Postgres transaction already solves.

When it stops being one transaction

The moment Ana and Bruno live in different databases. You can't get both aggregate updates into one commit, so you introduce the clearing account. Leg A (Ana → clearing) is one atomic commit in Ana's shard with all its own rows. Leg B (clearing → Bruno) is another in Bruno's. Each leg is atomic on its own; the transfer as a whole is a saga with a durable progress record. The invariant "every leg is one atomic commit" never changes. Only the number of legs does.

Interview sentence

"In one commit I write the transaction, its entries, both account aggregates with a version check, the idempotency record and the outbox row. Splitting any of them creates a specific financial bug: double-posting, an unexplained balance, or a lost event. One atomic boundary is the cheapest place to prove correctness."

5. Why success returns from the commit

Why does the ledger return success from the database commit and not from Kafka?

Because "success" has to mean something the bank can stand behind, and only the commit makes that true.

What Ana is being told

When her app shows "Sent", the promise is: your balance went down, Bruno's went up, and a crash cannot undo either. The database commit is the exact moment that promise becomes true. Before it, nothing has happened. After it, both entries, both balances and the idempotency record are on disk together. There is no more honest point to say "done".

Kafka receiving the event adds nothing to that promise. The event is a report that the transfer happened. Waiting for the report before confirming the fact is backwards.

What goes wrong if you wait for Kafka

  • Availability coupling. Kafka slow → every transfer slow. Kafka down → every transfer fails, even though the ledger is healthy and the money already moved. You've made a messaging outage into a banking outage.
  • Lying to the customer. Worse: the commit succeeded, Kafka timed out, so you return an error. Ana sees "failed", but R$10 has left her account. Her app thinks the first attempt failed, so it retries with a new key, and now she has sent R$20. Returning failure after a real commit is the most dangerous thing a ledger can do.
  • Latency for no gain. A synchronous publish adds a network round trip to a broker on the critical path, for a step the customer doesn't need before seeing "Sent."

What goes wrong if you return success before the commit

The mirror mistake: acknowledge the request, drop it on a queue, process later. Now "Sent" means "we'll try". If the consumer later finds insufficient funds, you have to walk back a confirmation you already gave. For money that is only acceptable when the product has explicitly designed a Processing state, which is exactly what you do for external rails, where you genuinely don't control the outcome.

The rule this protects

Success is a business state, not an HTTP 200. Define it as "the local financial commitment required by the product promise is durable." For an internal transfer that's debit and credit committed. For a PIX-out it's the debit plus a durable Processing record: you return "accepted", not "completed", because the external leg isn't yours to promise.

In the diagram: commit → return success → outbox worker publishes → Kafka → projections. The customer's response leaves at step two. Everything after is asynchronous and can be late without ever making step two false.

Interview sentence

"Money becomes durable at the commit; that is the only moment where 'success' is true and irreversible. Events describe that fact afterwards, so tying the customer's answer to Kafka would couple availability to messaging and risk telling the customer 'failed' after money already moved."

6. The outbox worker, and Kafka down for hours

How does the outbox worker work, and what happens if Kafka is down for hours? (Asked twice.)

How the outbox worker works

The outbox is just a table in the same database as the ledger. When the transfer commits, one of the rows written in that same transaction is:

outbox(id, event_type, payload, aggregate_id, created_at, published_at = NULL)

Because it is in the same commit, the rule is airtight: never a money movement without its outbox row, never an outbox row without its money movement.

A separate worker runs a loop:

  1. Read a batch of rows where published_at IS NULL, oldest first (ordered by an increasing id, so it's a queue).
  2. Publish each one to Kafka, using the account or transaction id as the partition key so events for one account stay ordered.
  3. When Kafka acknowledges, mark the row published (or delete it).
  4. If the publish fails, do nothing. The row stays unpublished and gets picked up next loop.

That's it. The worker is dumb on purpose. Its only job is "keep retrying until Kafka says yes."

  • Duplicates are expected. The worker can crash after Kafka acked but before it marked the row, and on restart it republishes. So the guarantee is at-least-once, and every consumer must be idempotent, usually by remembering the last event id it applied. Nubank said exactly this at QCon: they don't rely on exactly-once; every consumer dedupes on a correlation id.
  • One worker per partition, or a lease. Two workers scanning the same table double-publish constantly. Run one instance per ledger shard, or have workers claim rows with a short lease (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED is the usual Postgres trick).

Kafka down for six hours

Nothing about money breaks. Ana's transfer committed, her balance is right, Bruno's balance is right, a retry with the same key still returns the same answer. Customers can keep transferring the whole time.

What does break is everything downstream of Kafka:

  • The transaction feed in the app is stale: Bruno doesn't see the incoming R$10 in his history yet (his balance is right, because that's read from the ledger).
  • Push notifications don't go out.
  • Fraud scoring, analytics and any integrations see nothing.
  • The outbox table grows for six hours. At a few hundred TPS that is millions of rows: fine for a database, but watch disk, and plan backpressure if it goes on for days.

When Kafka returns, the worker drains the backlog oldest-first. Ordering is preserved per partition key, consumers dedupe, and the feed catches up. Nobody edits anything by hand.

What pages on-call

Not "Kafka is down"; the Kafka team already knows. The ledger team alerts on the age of the oldest unpublished outbox row. That one number catches a Kafka outage, a stuck worker, a bad batch that keeps failing, everything. Pair it with backlog count for the trend.

The trap the interviewer is setting

They want to see if you'll say "roll back the transfer because the event didn't go out." Never. Committed money is a fact; the event is a description of the fact, and descriptions can be late. The reverse trap is making the customer's success depend on Kafka being up, which turns a messaging outage into a banking outage.

Interview sentence

"Money remains correct; feeds and notifications go stale. The outbox backlog grows, publication resumes in order when Kafka returns, and consumers deduplicate. I alert on outbox age, not on Kafka health."

7. Why Kafka is not the source of truth

Why is Kafka not the source of truth in the ledger design?

Because Kafka can't give you the one thing a ledger needs: a single atomic decision that covers the balance check, the entries, the idempotency record and the new balance together.

What "source of truth" has to do

When Ana sends R$10, someone has to decide, in one indivisible step: does she have it, has this command already been processed, and if both are fine, write the two entries and the new balances. Split any part of that from the rest and a crash in the gap creates money that moved twice, or a balance that doesn't match its history.

A relational database does this natively. That is what a transaction with a unique constraint and a conditional update is.

Kafka is an append-only log of messages. It is excellent at durably recording "this happened" and fanning it out in order. It has no concept of "check Ana's balance and reject if insufficient", no unique constraint on an idempotency key, no conditional write against a version. To get those you would build a consumer that reads the log, keeps state and enforces the rules. At that point the consumer's store is the source of truth, and Kafka is just the pipe into it.

Three concrete failures if Kafka were the truth

  1. Two concurrent debits. Both producers publish "debit Ana 80". Kafka happily stores both. Nobody rejected the second one, because nothing in the publish path knew Ana's balance. The overdraft is now durably recorded and you find out later.
  2. Duplicate delivery. Producers retry and consumers rebalance, so the same message can appear twice. Kafka's "exactly-once" covers Kafka-to-Kafka processing and stops at your database write. You still need idempotency in the consumer: the hard problem moved, it didn't go away.
  3. Availability coupling. If "success" means "Kafka acked the message", then a Kafka outage becomes a banking outage. If "success" means "the database committed", Kafka can be down for hours and customers keep transferring.

The honest version of event sourcing

An interviewer may push: "Nubank is event-driven, so isn't event sourcing the truth?" Fair. An event-sourced ledger is possible. But the events still need per-account ordering, a uniqueness check on command identity, snapshots and controlled replay, and the component that enforces those is a database with an atomic write boundary, not the bus. Nubank's own description matches: the Datomic transaction function is where check-and-write is one ACID operation; Kafka carries messages between services, and every consumer dedupes on a correlation id.

The division of labor

Database commitThe decision. Money becomes durable here.
Outbox rowWritten in that same commit: the promise to tell others.
KafkaThe distribution: feeds, notifications, fraud, analytics, other services.
ProjectionsBuilt from Kafka; rebuildable. If one drifts you replay from the journal, never the other way around.

Interview sentence

"Kafka distributes facts; it can't provide the local atomic boundary that ties the balance check, the entries and the idempotency key into one decision. The database commit is the business decision and the outbox publishes it afterwards."

Avoid saying

"Kafka gives us exactly-once so we're safe." It doesn't, and the interviewer will know.

8. Projections, and what happens when one drifts

What are projections in the ledger design, and what happens if one drifts from the journal?

What a projection is

A read model built from the ledger's events, shaped for one question. The journal (transaction plus entries) is the truth, but it is shaped for correctness, not for reading. Nobody wants to sum ten years of entries to render a transaction list.

So you consume the events Kafka delivers and build derived views:

  • Transaction feed: "show Bruno his last 50 movements", denormalized with the counterparty name and a description.
  • Notification state: what has been pushed, to whom.
  • Search / Ops index: find a transfer by reference, amount, date range.
  • Analytics and fraud features: spend per merchant per day, velocity counters.
  • Monthly statement: a snapshot rebuilt from entries, then frozen.

Each can live in whatever store suits it (Postgres, Elasticsearch, a columnar warehouse), be eventually consistent, and be thrown away and rebuilt. That last property is the whole point: a projection is a cache of a computation over the journal.

The one thing that is not a projection

The account aggregate (posted, held, available, version). It looks like a derived view, since it is a sum over entries. But it is updated in the same commit as the entries, and it is read to authorise spending. That makes it authoritative write state, not a projection. If the interviewer asks "isn't the balance just a projection?", the answer is: conceptually yes, operationally no. It lives inside the write boundary because you can't approve a debit from something eventually consistent.

Drift: two very different situations

A true projection drifts (feed shows a transfer Bruno didn't receive, statement total is off by R$10):

  • Nobody's money is wrong. The journal is right; the view is stale or buggy.
  • Cause is usually a skipped or double-applied event, a consumer bug, or a replay that started at the wrong offset.
  • Fix: rebuild. Truncate the projection, replay events from the journal (or from the start of Kafka if retention allows), let it catch up. Consumers are idempotent, so replay is safe.
  • Never fix a projection by editing ledger entries. That changes the truth to match the copy.
  • Detect drift continuously: a reconciliation job compares projection totals against journal totals per account per day, and alerts on any mismatch.

The account aggregate disagrees with the journal (available says 70, entries sum to 60):

  • This is an incident, not a stale cache. Something authorised spending against a wrong number, or a write path bypassed the transaction.
  • Isolate writes on the affected accounts first. You don't keep approving debits from a balance you know is wrong.
  • Recompute the expected aggregate from entries. Investigate how the gap appeared: a bug, a manual edit, a partial failover.
  • Repair through an audited command with a reason code and four-eyes approval, never UPDATE account SET balance = 60. The repair itself is a recorded event.
  • Reconcile again to confirm. Then add the detection that would have caught it earlier: sequence numbers per account, a totals check on every commit or on a short cadence.

Interview sentence

"Projections are rebuildable read models fed by the outbox events; if one drifts, I replay from the journal and never touch entries. The account aggregate is different: it's inside the atomic write boundary because it authorizes spending, so a mismatch there is an incident: isolate, recompute, audited repair, then reconcile."

The trap

Treating those two as the same. Saying "just rebuild the balance from Kafka" tells the interviewer you'd authorize debits from an eventually consistent source.

  10. "Two billion transactions a month is about eight thousand a second at peak and thirty thousand writes; that's past one primary, so partitioning by customer is on the roadmap from day one, and clearing handles the cross-customer case."Numbers that end in a decision.
  11. "My single point of failure is the write authority's primary: synchronous replica, automated failover, and during the window I reject new debits rather than approve from a replica. Failover time is my availability ceiling."Assume things will fail.
  12. "Events carry a schema version, consumers accept N and N+1 during a rollout, and entries are never rewritten; the aggregate is rebuilt if its shape changes."Schema evolution.

Three things never to say

  • "Kafka gives us exactly-once." It doesn't; your ledger makes the effect happen once.
  • "Roll back the transfer because the event didn't go out." Committed money is a fact.
  • "Just rebuild the balance from Kafka." That authorizes debits from an eventually consistent source.
  • "It'll be a lot of data." Say the number and what it decides.

10. Follow-up answers

The eight follow-ups the interviewer is most likely to push on, each answered the way you'd say it, then the reasoning behind it.

Row lock versus version

Short answer. Both serialise concurrent writes to one account; they differ in when they pay for it. A row lock (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE) pays up front: the second transaction waits for the first to commit, then sees the new balance. A version pays on conflict: both read freely, the second one's conditional update matches zero rows, rolls back, and retries. I default to the version because ledger contention is low and I never want a long-held lock spanning anything slow. I'd switch to locking for a hot account, where retry storms cost more than waiting.

Why. Under optimistic concurrency a lost race costs one wasted read-validate cycle. Under pessimistic locking every race costs the loser waiting time and costs the database a lock to manage, and if any code path holds that lock across a network call the whole account stalls. Locks also allow deadlocks between transactions that touch two accounts; versions can't. The version is also a free correctness check on the aggregate: any write that bypasses the versioned update path stands out.

Alternative. A per-account command queue (one consumer per account key) gives ordering without either mechanism; it's what you reach for when contention is high and you already have partitioned messaging.

Why not timestamps

Short answer. A timestamp says when a machine thought something happened. It cannot say whether two writes conflicted. Two servers can stamp two debits with times microseconds apart in either direction, and neither stamp stops both from committing. The version is a per-account counter that advances only on commit, so it is the authoritative order of that account's history. Wall-clock time is metadata: useful for reporting, not for correctness.

Why. Clocks drift, jump backwards after NTP corrections, and differ across regions. A balance is a cumulative sum over ordered entries, so a wrong order is a wrong balance. Versions also handle late-arriving events cleanly: an event that shows up out of order is replayed into the sequence, not patched into place by its timestamp.

Deterministic lock ordering for multi-account transactions

Short answer. When one transaction touches two accounts, always acquire them in the same order, for example by ascending account id. Then Ana→Bruno and Bruno→Ana both lock Ana's row first, one waits for the other, and neither can deadlock.

Why. A deadlock needs a cycle: transaction 1 holds A and wants B while transaction 2 holds B and wants A. If every transaction acquires resources in one global order, no cycle can form. With versions instead of locks, the equivalent rule is to check both versions in the same commit. There is nothing to deadlock on, but you still read both accounts before writing either, so the conditional updates see a consistent pair.

Interview follow-up. "What about a transaction with five legs, fees and tax?" Same rule: sort all account ids, lock in that order, write all entries, check they sum to zero, commit.

What a conflicting payload on a reused key returns

Short answer. A deterministic conflict error, typically HTTP 409 or an explicit IDEMPOTENCY_KEY_CONFLICT, that says "this key was already used with a different request" and includes the original transaction id for reference. The new payload is not processed, and the stored result is not overwritten.

Why. The key is the client's promise that "same key" means "same intention". If the payload differs, the client broke the promise, and the ledger has no safe way to know which intention is real. Processing the new one could move money the client never meant to move again. Overwriting the old result would lie about what already happened. Rejecting both is the only outcome that can't cause financial harm, and it surfaces the client bug immediately instead of hiding it.

How. Store a hash of the canonicalised payload alongside the key. On arrival with a known key, compare hashes: equal, return the stored transaction; different, conflict. The comparison happens inside the same transaction as the lookup, so two concurrent arrivals with the same new key resolve through the unique constraint, not through a race.

Why the aggregate is not a projection

Short answer. Because it authorizes spending. A projection is a read model that may lag and can be rebuilt; the account aggregate (posted, held, available, version) is written in the same commit as the entries and is read to decide whether a debit is allowed. Conceptually it's a sum over entries; operationally it's part of the authoritative write state.

Why. "Never debit from stale funds" only holds if the number you check is the number the commit is about to change. If the aggregate were an eventually consistent projection, two debits could both read an old balance and both be approved before it caught up. Keeping it in the write boundary is what turns the funds check into a conditional write. The cost: every posting updates the aggregate row, so a busy account contends on itself. The version handles that.

The tell. A rebuildable thing is a projection. The aggregate can be rebuilt from entries, but if you ever have to, that's an incident with isolate, recompute, audited repair, reconcile, not a routine replay.

What the outbox alert is

Short answer. Age of the oldest unpublished outbox row, paired with backlog count for trend. Page when age crosses the threshold that would make projections unacceptably stale for the product, not on Kafka health.

Why. That single number covers every way publication can stop: Kafka down, worker crashed, a poison row that fails every retry, a lease that never expires, a partition with no consumer. Kafka health alone misses all but the first. Backlog count alone misses a stuck head-of-queue row when volume is low. Age of the oldest row catches both.

The rest of the on-call board. Stuck transfer age, clearing imbalance and age of the oldest clearing item, posting error rate, account-version conflict rate, reconciliation mismatch count, replica and failover health.

Why 2PC is an option to evaluate rather than a taboo

Short answer. Two-phase commit does deliver what the clearing account can't: an all-or-nothing result across two databases with no visible intermediate state. I'd evaluate it when the platform supports it reliably and the product genuinely cannot accept a Processing state. I'd avoid it as the default across independent partitions because it blocks participants while they wait for the coordinator, has painful recovery when the coordinator dies mid-protocol, and is weakly supported across heterogeneous services. Clearing is my default because its in-between state is explicit, observable and operable.

Why the interviewer asks. They're checking whether you reason from criteria or from slogans. "2PC is bad" is a slogan. The criteria are: correctness (both give it), latency (2PC adds a round trip and a prepare phase), blocking (2PC holds locks on both sides while it coordinates), operability (a stuck 2PC transaction needs manual resolution of in-doubt state; a stuck clearing item is a visible row with an age), and customer experience (2PC has no Processing state; clearing does). The one factor that can flip the decision is whether Product can accept Processing.

Same reasoning, different context. If they ask why a real-time payment rail doesn't use 2PC: payer and payee are in different write authorities, so each leg is made locally atomic and idempotent, progress is kept durable, and a later leg that can't finish is compensated with a new posting.

How the clearing account keeps a cross-shard transfer accounted for between legs

Short answer. At every moment, every unit of money has exactly one owner. Before leg A, Ana owns R$10. After leg A commits, the clearing account owns R$10 and Ana's entries and aggregate say so. After leg B commits, Bruno owns it. There is never a moment where the R$10 has left Ana and not yet arrived anywhere; it is sitting, visibly, in clearing.

Before:   Ana 100          Clearing   0          Bruno 50      total 150
Leg A:    Ana  90          Clearing  10          Bruno 50      total 150   ← customer sees Processing
Leg B:    Ana  90          Clearing   0          Bruno 60      total 150   ← Completed

What makes it safe.

  • Each leg is one atomic commit in its own shard, with its own entries, aggregate updates, idempotency key and outbox row. Everything from section 4 applies to each leg.
  • Per-leg keys: transferId:legA and transferId:legB, enforced uniquely on the shard that owns the leg. A worker retrying leg B after a timeout gets the prior posting back, never a second credit.
  • A durable transfer record says which leg is done. The transfer state advances only after the destination shard acknowledges the durable commit of leg B.
  • The customer sees the truth: the payer sees the debit with the transfer marked Processing; the receiver does not see funds yet. Neither side sees Completed before leg B is durable. Define a processing SLA and an escalation path for it.
  • If leg B can never complete, after bounded retries and dependency recovery, policy decides: keep pending, route to manual repair, or reverse leg A with a new linked posting that moves the R$10 from clearing back to Ana. The committed debit is never deleted, and you never compensate automatically while the external outcome is still unknown.
  • Clearing is monitored like a customer account: scoped by currency, rail and possibly partition so no single row becomes globally hot; every item aged; reconciled gross and net; alerts on both total amount and age of the oldest item. Every clearing entry maps back to a transfer id, so the balance is always explainable.

The interview sentence. "Between legs the money is in clearing, which is a real account with real entries, so the books balance at every instant. The customer sees Processing rather than a lie, and if leg B fails we compensate with a new posting, never by deleting the debit."

How the schema evolves after launch without downtime

Short answer. Add fields, never repurpose them. New fields are optional with a default. Deploy the schema change before the code that needs it, then the code that reads both shapes, then migrate, then remove the old path: expand, migrate, contract. Events carry a schema version; consumers accept N and N+1 during a rollout. Entries are never rewritten to fit a new shape; if the aggregate's shape changes, it is rebuilt from the entries. Code rolls back; data doesn't.

Why the interviewer asks. Nubank's prep document lists schema evolution under data and storage, and it separates people who have run a system from people who have drawn one. The trap is "we'd migrate the table", which on a ledger means a lock on the hot path or a rewrite of history.

What you cache, and what you never cache

Short answer. Cache what is read far more than written and tolerant of being slightly stale: versioned reference data, the customer's rendered feed, fee tables under a version key. Never cache the available balance for a funds check, and never authorise from a read replica; both are a stale balance with a different name. Invalidate by version where you can (a new fee table is a new key), by event where you must, and by TTL only for display data. Saying what you won't cache is the strong part of the answer.

11. When they push on scalability and performance

A Lead candidate who did not pass in late 2025 reported questions mostly about scalability and performance. Candidates in 2026 describe an interruption roughly every ten minutes: "volume goes up tenfold", "a dependency is down", "reproduce a balance as of three months ago". This is how to take those for the ledger.

The numbers, worked out loud

Nubank's prep document expects rough arithmetic that ends in a decision. For the ledger, the arithmetic changes the answer: unlike chargebacks, this one does outgrow a single primary.

2B transactions / month ≈ 770 / s average, ~8k / s peak (Black Friday, payday)
2 entries per transaction + 1 aggregate update + 1 outbox row ≈ 4 writes  → ~30k writes / s peak
Entries 200 bytes × 4B / month ≈ 0.8 TB / month on the hot path
Read: balance checks ≈ same as transactions; feed reads 10× that, served from projections

The decision. Thirty thousand writes per second at peak is beyond one comfortable Postgres primary (thousands of simple writes per second, tens of thousands of indexed reads, comfortable up to a few TB), so partitioning by customer is on the roadmap from day one, and the failover time of each partition is the availability ceiling. Cross-customer transfers then need the clearing account. Nearly a terabyte a month of entries means checkpoints and cold archiving are not optional. Analytics never touches the transactional store. Say the number that would change your mind: "if peak were under a few thousand writes a second I'd stay on one primary and measure."

Begin simple, then iterate: the ledger as v1 → v2 → v3

Nubank's prep document wants a first idea, its flaws found by you, and the fixes in order. For the ledger the storyline is short, and the end state of v2 is the section 1 diagram.

v1: a balance column updated in place. One service reads a balance, subtracts, writes it back, and publishes an event from memory. Say the pros: one table, one query, obviously fast. Then the four flaws, in the order they would hurt.

v2: fix correctness. Entries explain the balance and make "as of a date" a query (flaw 1). The account version turns the funds check into a conditional write (flaw 2). A client-generated key stored in the same commit makes a retry return the same transaction (flaw 3). An outbox row in that commit means an event exists if and only if the money moved, and success never waits on Kafka (flaw 4). Say what it costs: four or five rows per transfer instead of one, a worker, and consumers that must dedupe.

v3: scale and operate, only when the numbers say so, and for the ledger they do. Partition by customer because customer-to-customer interaction is low, tag every transaction with the customer id from day one, and route cross-shard transfers through clearing as two legs. Move every non-authorising read to projections and analytics to its own environment. Checkpoint entries per account so a rebuild replays from the last checkpoint.

The five-step answer, every time

  1. Ask what grew. Transactions per second, accounts, entries per account, countries, or read traffic? "Tenfold" with no noun is a trap; supply the noun if they don't.
  2. Name the bottleneck with a number you'd measure. Posting p99, account-version conflict rate, oldest outbox row, replica lag, failover time, entries per account for the hottest account.
  3. Scale what scales. Everything idempotent and stateless: outbox publishers, projection builders, reconciliation jobs, read serving from projections.
  4. Say what doesn't, and why. One write authority per account (that's the invariant, not a limitation to apologise for), hot accounts, and the failover time of the authoritative store, which is your availability ceiling.
  5. Protect the invariants while you scale. Balanced entries, effectively-once, immutable history, never debit from a stale balance. Any "optimisation" that bends one of those is a bug with better latency.

"How would you improve performance?"

Usually means latency on the posting path. What is on that path: key lookup, account reads, funds check, conditional update, one commit. That's it. Publication, projections and notifications already happen after commit. If p99 is bad, look in this order: a hot account row (version conflicts and retries), a lock held across something slow (a network call inside the transaction is the classic), an unindexed key or account lookup, and only then the database itself. The biggest latency win in most real ledgers is taking a synchronous call out of the transaction, not tuning the database.

The three "improvements" that are actually bugs

  • "Cache the available balance." A cached balance is a stale balance, and authorising from it is exactly how money is spent twice. Cache display balances all you like; the funds check reads the authority.
  • "Authorise from a read replica." Same failure with a different name. Replica lag is a stale balance.
  • "Post asynchronously and confirm later." That turns "Sent" into "we'll try", which the product may accept for external rails with a Processing state, but never for an internal transfer.

Interview sentence

"I would start with one write authority and measure. When entries throughput or failover time crosses a threshold I set with load tests, I would partition by customer because customer-to-customer interaction is low, and I would tag every transaction with the customer id from day one so the split is possible later. Then cross-customer transfers become the explicit hard problem, handled with clearing and compensation."

Avoid saying

"Add more instances" without naming instances of what. "Cache the balance." "Read replicas for authorisation." "Shard everything" without a key. "Move to NoSQL" as if the store were the problem. Each answers a generic question the interviewer didn't ask.

Their own database makes the same trade explicit. The Datomic overview states two properties side by side: trivial read scaling, because immutable databases can be copied to any number of readers, and no per-database write scaling, because transactions are totally serialised. That is why Nubank shards by customer, and why cross-shard movement is the part to spend care on. Section 11 of Nubank's prep document, decoded has the source and the two posts behind it.

Say it once, in their own words

"Your own database makes this trade explicit: Datomic gives you immutability and free read scaling in exchange for serialized writes per database, which is exactly why you shard by customer, and exactly why cross-shard movement is the part I'd spend the most care on."

Reads of the display balance and the feed keep working from projections. Failover time is the availability ceiling, and you say so.

Security and abuse, in one breath. The ledger accepts postings only from authenticated callers with a scoped command set and limits per caller, so a dispute platform can issue a provisional credit but not an arbitrary transfer. Every posting records the caller and the business key. Nobody edits entries, engineers included; a correction is a new linked posting with an approval. Idempotency keys are scoped per caller so one caller cannot replay another's, and a replayed request returns the original transaction, never a second effect. Rate limits live at the edge, not in the ledger.

Monitoring and debugging in one breath

"One correlation id per command, on every log line, event and posting. I measure posting p99, version-conflict rate per account, oldest outbox row, replica lag, clearing balance and the age of its oldest item, and reconciliation mismatches. I alert on customer and money impact, not on raw errors. For one transfer, I read its entries and events; for many, I read the dependency dashboards."
